# A History of Emotions

Ask someone how many basic emotions there are and, in most of the English-speaking world today, you'll get a fast answer: six, or thereabouts — the ones a Pixar film taught a generation of children to name. It sounds less like an opinion than a fact, the kind of thing biology simply confirms once you look. It is neither. It's the most recent chapter in a much longer argument — one that Aristotle, the Stoics, a medieval friar, three Enlightenment philosophers who couldn't agree with each other, a Sanskrit dramaturge, a Han-dynasty physician, and a twentieth-century anthropologist on a Pacific atoll all took a turn at, each producing a genuinely different answer to the same question. This course follows that argument.

Every school catalogued in this library — Plutchik's wheel, Nonviolent Communication's needs, the Stoics' *pathē*, Bharata's *rasa* — presents its own categories as though they were simply *there*, waiting to be named. They aren't. What counts as one feeling, how many there are, what axis organizes them, whether "emotion" is even the right container — has been rebuilt from scratch by every tradition that has taken up the problem, ancient to contemporary. That instability is not a flaw in the record. It's why this library can hold Aristotle, Bharata, Aquinas, Descartes, Darwin, Ekman, and Barrett side by side without pretending any of them secretly agree with one another — and it's the argument this whole library runs on: **there are no fixed biological fingerprints for emotions, and people still have to make meaning, so every school here is a working interface, not a discovery.**

## Five traditions, not "Europe and the rest"

It would be easy to tell this as "the European tradition" on one side and "everything else" on the other. That framing is avoided here on purpose — it would make Europe the measure and file every other tradition as a variation or a comparison case, defined by what it isn't rather than what it is. So what follows are **five lineages**, each followed on its own terms and in its own internal chronology: **Greek, Latin-Christian, and European** (the longest continuous written record, from Aristotle through contemporary constructionism); **Sanskrit** (*rasa*, from Bharata through Abhinavagupta); **Chinese** (*qíng* and the doctrine of the seven emotions); **Amazonian** (Amerindian perspectivism, which turns out not to be organized around a taxonomy of feelings at all — and that difference is itself the finding, not a gap); and **Micronesian** (Catherine Lutz's ethnography of Ifaluk atoll). The European lineage gets the most space below not because it's the reference case the others are measured against, but because it happens to be the one with the longest continuous written record — length and documentation, not primacy. Where the lineages actually crossed — through translation, colonial contact, and the shared instruments of modern cross-cultural psychology — gets its own short section near the end, because contact is a real historical fact and "parallel" shouldn't be read as "sealed."

## Greek, Latin-Christian, and European: a lineage that never agreed with its own past

### Aristotle: feelings as levers, not a list

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* doesn't offer a taxonomy of emotion in the modern sense. It's a handbook for orators: a *pathos* is "that on account of which people change and differ in their judgments" — anger, pity, fear, and their opposites, each analyzed by the situation that provokes it and the grounds on which it can be talked up or down ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on ancient theories of emotion](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2014/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD1Background.html); [an overview of Aristotle's treatment of the *pathē*](https://sites.dwrl.utexas.edu/davis/courses/rhe-330e-pathos/197-2/aristotle-on-the-pathe/)). His *Nicomachean Ethics* gives a broader, differently membered list for a different purpose. There was never one canonical Aristotelian emotion list — the count and membership shift with what the text is trying to do. That instability is the first data point in this whole genealogy: the unit was never fixed, even within one philosopher's own writing.

The Stoics, starting with Chrysippus, rebuilt the apparatus around an entirely different question: not *what moves an audience* but *what a mistaken judgment does to the soul*. Four *pathē* — distress, fear, appetite, pleasure — sit against three *eupatheiai*, rational "good feelings" available to the sage: joy, wish, and caution ([Stoic passions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoic_passions); [Chrysippus's *On Passions*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Passions); [on *eupatheia*](https://viastoica.com/what-is-eupatheia/)). The organizing axis here is neither valence nor a body-part inventory — it's epistemic: is the judgment behind the feeling true or false. Read the Stoic account here: [Greek Pathē — Rhetoric and Stoic Ethics](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/greek-pathe/grammar.json).

### Aquinas: ordered by arduousness, not pleasantness

Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae*, inherits Aristotle's *pathē* and reorganizes eleven passions around the soul's two sensitive appetites: six concupiscible passions (love/hatred, desire/aversion, joy/sorrow) respond to good or evil taken simply; five irascible passions (hope/despair, fear/daring, and anger, which Aquinas holds has no opposite) respond to good or evil apprehended as *arduous* — difficult to reach or avoid ([*Summa Theologiae* I-II, Q.23](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2023.htm)). This is worth stating plainly because it's easy to read past: **the organizing axis is not valence.** Hope and fear sit together not because they feel similarly pleasant or unpleasant, but because both concern a good or evil that is *hard to reach*. A taxonomy this different from a valence axis sitting at the intellectual center of medieval Christian moral psychology for centuries is itself evidence against treating "pleasant vs. unpleasant" as a natural, self-evident way to sort feeling. See [Aquinas's Eleven Passions](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/aquinas-passions/grammar.json).

### Three incompatible "basic" lists in one century

**Descartes**, in *Les passions de l'âme* (1649), arrives at six *primitive* passions — wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy, sadness — from which every other passion is compounded ([overview of the treatise's structure](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Passions_de_l%27%C3%A2me)). It's tempting, three centuries later, to hear "six basic passions" and think of Ekman's six basic emotions. Don't: Descartes has wonder and desire; Ekman has surprise, disgust, and fear. Only love/joy and hatred/sadness are even loosely analogous, and Descartes is explicit that his six are *causal primitives* — mechanical building blocks — not a claim about universal cross-cultural recognition. The idea of "six basic passions" has a European philosophical pedigree that predates Ekman by roughly three hundred years, and it was never the same six.

**Spinoza**, writing in explicit dialogue with (and often against) Descartes, reduces the primitives further, to three: desire, joy, and sadness — defined not as felt qualities but as transitions in the body's power of acting ([Spinoza's theory of the emotions, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD5Spinoza.html)). **Hume**, in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), cuts along a different seam entirely: *direct* passions (desire, grief, joy, hope, fear) arise immediately from pleasure or pain, while *indirect* passions (pride, love, hatred) additionally require an idea of self or another person as the passion's object ([Hume on the passions, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD8Hume.html)). Three major European thinkers, working within about ninety years of one another, produced three structurally incompatible taxonomies with three different primitive counts. This is the clearest evidence in the pre-modern record that "the basic emotions" was never a discovery converged on from different angles — it's a design choice, remade by each thinker to fit the argument at hand. Read Descartes's own list at [Descartes's Six Primitive Passions](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/descartes-passions/grammar.json).

### How "emotion" itself got invented

This is the load-bearing claim of the whole European lineage. Thomas Dixon's *From Passions to Emotions: The Creation of a Secular Psychological Category* (2003) argues that "the emotions" — one unifying English category covering everything from a startle reflex to religious rapture — is a nineteenth-century invention, not a discovery of something that had always been there under older names ([Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/from-passions-to-emotions/9E152739407029F882D0384D0670E49F); [review, *Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jhbs.20028)). Before this, English distinguished **passions** (strong, morally dangerous movements of the appetite, in the Aristotelian-Thomist line), **affections** (steadier, morally approved inclinations — love of God, natural affection for kin), and **sentiments** (Scottish Enlightenment vocabulary for refined, socially calibrated feeling). Scottish thinkers, and then increasingly secular, physicalist writers after them, collapsed these into one supposedly neutral, scientifically tractable class: "the emotions." If Dixon is right, the very container twentieth-century psychology set out to find universal biological markers *for* is itself a relatively recent, English-language, secularizing artifact — which means universality claims about that container's contents are being asked to do work before the container's own boundaries have ever been justified.

### Darwin and James: two rival late-nineteenth-century foundations

Darwin's *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) argues that facial and bodily expressions are largely innate and adaptive — evidenced by their appearance in infants, in people born blind, and in nonhuman animals ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Expression_of_the_Emotions_in_Man_and_Animals)). Darwin supplemented direct observation with a network of questionnaires sent to missionaries and colonial travelers — an early, methodologically loose ancestor of the cross-cultural "universality" study design that would be formalized a century later. William James, in 1884, proposed the opposite causal order from ordinary intuition: we don't tremble because we're afraid, we're afraid because we tremble. Bodily changes come first; the *feeling* of them is the emotion ([full text of "What Is an Emotion?"](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/emotion.htm)). James's theory is a direct ancestor of the interoception-forward accounts that reappear at the end of this lineage, though he located emotion in specific, differentiated bodily patterns per emotion — where the later constructionist account locates it in one much more general signal, categorized after the fact. Darwin's own account, presented in his own words where the public-domain text allows, lives at [Darwin — The Expression of the Emotions](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/darwin-expression/grammar.json).

### Tomkins, Ekman, Plutchik: the affect-programme era

Silvan Tomkins's *Affect Imagery Consciousness* (1962) proposed nine innate affects as hard-wired subcortical programs ([overview](https://neuroanthropology.net/2008/06/23/affect-at-the-interface-silvan-tomkins/)). Tomkins mentored a generation of researchers — Paul Ekman among them — who took the idea forward into cross-cultural facial-expression research. Ekman narrowed Tomkins's nine to six — anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise — and, with Wallace Friesen, built the **Facial Action Coding System (FACS)**, an anatomically grounded notation for facial-muscle movement, published in 1978 ([Ekman's own history of FACS](https://paulekman.medium.com/the-history-of-the-facial-action-coding-system-facs-8787a86ed81f)). Philosopher Paul Griffiths named the resulting package the **affect-programme model**: an automatic appraisal mechanism triggers one of a small set of discrete, coordinated programs, subject to learned cultural "display rules" that can mask but not eliminate the underlying program ([overview](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.7208/9780226488738-003/html)). Robert Plutchik, working independently, proposed a different eight — joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation — arranged as a wheel of four opposite pairs ([overview of Plutchik's wheel](https://www.6seconds.org/2025/02/06/plutchik-wheel-emotions/)). Plutchik's eight and Ekman's six overlap on five terms but aren't the same list, built from different premises, published within a few years of each other — even inside the mid-twentieth-century "basic emotions" consensus, there was no actual consensus on the count or the membership, only on the *architecture*. Both are represented here in their own voice: [Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/plutchik-wheel/grammar.json) and [Basic-Emotion / Affect-Programme Theory](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/ekman-basic/grammar.json).

### The dimensional and constructionist turn

James Russell's 1980 circumplex model discarded discrete categories in favor of two continuous dimensions — pleasure/displeasure and arousal/sleepiness — onto which any feeling word can be plotted ([overview](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Motivation_and_emotion/Book/2019/Circumplex_model_of_affect)). Lisa Feldman Barrett's **theory of constructed emotion**, synthesized in *How Emotions Are Made* (2017), proposes that emotion isn't a triggered program but a real-time construction: the brain generates a general, undifferentiated **core affect** signal from interoceptive and exteroceptive input, and *categorizes* that signal — using concepts learned from language and culture — into an instance of "fear" or "shame" or whatever the moment and the person's vocabulary make available ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_constructed_emotion); [Barrett's own description](https://lisafeldmanbarrett.com/books/how-emotions-are-made/)). This is the constructionist account this whole library treats as its substrate — not because it's the last word, but because "no fixed biological fingerprint" is where the record, read honestly, actually points. Read it in its own terms at [Constructed Emotion — the Substrate](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/constructed-emotion/grammar.json).

## Sanskrit: *rasa* as an event, not a state

Bharata Muni's *Nāṭyaśāstra* (composed roughly between the 2nd century BCE and the 2nd century CE) sets out eight *rasas* — the erotic (*śṛṅgāra*), comic (*hāsya*), pathetic (*karuṇā*), furious (*raudra*), heroic (*vīra*), fearful (*bhayānaka*), odious (*bībhatsa*), and marvelous (*adbhuta*) ([overview of the *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s rasa theory](https://caleidoscope.in/featured/rasas-in-bharata-munis-natya-shastra); [course notes on Bharata's rasa theory](https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202004120632194631nishi_Rasa_Theory.pdf)). This is a genuinely different kind of category than anything in the Greek or European lineage: **a rasa is not, in the first instance, a state inside a person's body.** It's what a well-made performance produces in a spectator — an aesthetic "flavor" distilled out of a character's felt emotion. The unit of analysis is the relationship between performer, text, and audience, not an individual's physiology.

Roughly a millennium later, the philosopher Abhinavagupta, commenting on the *Nāṭyaśāstra*, argued Bharata's eight were incomplete and added a ninth, *śānta* (peace) — arguing, characteristically for his nondual framework, that *śānta* is not merely one rasa among nine but the ground the other eight relish against, likened to the string that gives a jeweled necklace its form even though no one admires the string itself ([summary of Abhinavagupta's addition of *śānta*-rasa](https://ijcrt.org/papers/IJCRT2010483.pdf)). Whether *śānta* is best read as a ninth peer category or as something closer to a substrate underneath the other eight is itself a live question within the tradition — a structural echo, worth naming without overstating the parallel, of this library's own claim that constructed emotion is a *frame* rather than a peer school. Read Bharata's and Abhinavagupta's own categories at [Rasa — Bharata's Aesthetics of Performance](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/rasa/grammar.json).

## Chinese: *qíng* and the seven emotions

Chinese medical and philosophical texts group feeling under *qíng* (情), a term broader than the English "emotion" — one that in classical usage also carries senses closer to "circumstance" or "the facts of a matter," so the container word itself doesn't partition cleanly along the same lines "emotion" does in English (a claim this account holds provisionally, pending a closer specialist source, rather than as settled fact). What's well documented is the clinical taxonomy: the *Huangdi Neijing* (compiled roughly during the Han dynasty) organizes feeling into the "seven emotions" (*qīqíng*, 七情) — joy, anger, worry, contemplation, grief, fear, and fright — each assigned a correspondence with one of the five viscera: the heart governs joy, the liver governs anger, the spleen governs contemplation, the lung governs grief, the kidney governs fear ([overview of the seven emotions](https://tcmwiki.com/wiki/seven-emotions); [encyclopedia entry](https://asante-academy.com/encyclopedia/seven-emotions/)). The organizing axis here is neither valence, nor Aristotle's judgment-alteration, nor Bharata's performer-audience relation — it's a physiological correspondence system, where an emotion in excess becomes a *pathogenic factor*: too much anger injures the liver, too much grief injures the lung. Feeling is folded directly into a theory of disease, not a theory of mind. Read the seven emotions in their own terms at [Qíng and the Seven Emotions](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/chinese-qing/grammar.json).

## Amazonian: a lineage that refuses the premise

The other four lineages here share a family resemblance this one deliberately doesn't: each produces a taxonomy — a list of *pathē*, a set of *rasas*, seven *qíng*, eleven Thomist passions, six Cartesian primitives, six Ekman affect programs — naming and counting the feelings a person can have. Amerindian perspectivism, as synthesized across decades of ethnography by the Brazilian anthropologist Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, doesn't produce a list of that kind, and that absence is itself the finding, not a gap in the record.

Across a wide range of Amazonian cosmologies, the being who feels, perceives, and acts — human, jaguar, spirit, or otherwise — is not distinguished from other kinds of being by *what* it feels or *how* it feels it. What differs is the **world each kind of body discloses.** A human sees blood; a jaguar, inhabiting the same event from its own bodily perspective, sees manioc beer — both perceptions correct, not because the two beings interpret one shared world differently, but because each kind of body opens onto its own *nature*, while all beings share a single generic subjective condition: the capacity to be a point of view at all ([Viveiros de Castro, "Perspectival Anthropology and the Method of Controlled Equivocation," 2004](https://digitalcommons.trinity.edu/tipiti/vol2/iss1/1/)). Viveiros de Castro calls this **multinaturalism**, in deliberate inversion of Western multiculturalism's terms: multiculturalism holds one nature and many cultures describing it differently; multinaturalism holds one culture — a shared condition of subjectivity, common to humans and non-humans alike — and many *natures*, as many worlds as there are kinds of body to disclose them ([overview of Amerindian perspectivism and multinaturalism](https://ea.fflch.usp.br/en/concept/amerindian-perspectivism)).

The consequence for a history of emotion specifically: asking "what are the six, or eight, or eleven emotions in Amazonian cosmology" isn't a question this material is positioned to answer, and forcing an answer would misrepresent it rather than fill a gap in it. Fear or hunger is not, on this account, first a private interior state that then gets sorted into a culturally specific bin — the move every other lineage above makes, in its own way. This library has no dedicated school for perspectivism yet — see it laid out as part of the [genealogy tree](../viewers/tree-viewer.html?src=../schools/genealogy-of-emotions/grammar.json) in the meantime.

## Micronesian: Ifaluk's *fago* and *song*

Catherine Lutz's *Unnatural Emotions: Everyday Sentiments on a Micronesian Atoll and Their Challenge to Western Theory* (1988), from fieldwork on Ifaluk atoll in 1977–78, documents an emotion vocabulary built on relational and moral terms that resist a clean mapping onto English ([overview of Lutz's Ifaluk fieldwork](https://www.academia.edu/54274341/Unnatural_Emotions_Everyday_Sentiments_on_a_Micronesian_Atoll_and_Their_Challenge_to_Western_Theory_Catherine_A_Lutz); [review of Lutz's ethnopsychological framework](https://lir.byuh.edu/index.php/pacific/article/download/2748/2660)). Two concepts anchor the record: ***fago***, often glossed "compassion," "love," or "sadness" depending on context — felt toward someone who is suffering, in need, or leaving, binding compassion, love, and a kind of grief together in a way English simply doesn't, because English's category boundaries were drawn along different lines; and ***song***, often glossed "anger," but specifically *justifiable* anger — anger felt in response to another's misconduct, sanctioned within Ifaluk moral order in a way undifferentiated English "anger" is not. Lutz's title is deliberately ironic: Ifaluk emotions only look "unnatural" from the starting assumption — her actual target — that English emotion categories are the natural, default baseline other cultures' categories are variations on. *Fago* and *song* are not private internal states dressed in local vocabulary; they're relational and moral concepts that don't survive being extracted from that context and relabeled with an English word chosen for rough resemblance.

Worth naming plainly: this is a single ethnographer's fieldwork record — rich, but not independently replicated by other fieldworkers — which is a different evidence tier from a canonical text with a long commentarial tradition behind it, the same evidence-tier discipline this library applies to every school of emotion it catalogues. See both concepts alongside the rest of the lineage at the [genealogy tree](../viewers/tree-viewer.html?src=../schools/genealogy-of-emotions/grammar.json).

## Where the lineages met

Five lineages followed separately is not the same claim as five lineages that never touched. Darwin's 1872 questionnaire to missionaries and colonial travelers is one of the earliest documented instances of the European lineage's science of emotion actively soliciting data from outside itself — and it set a template (a Western researcher's category list, checked against a non-Western population's behavior) that the twentieth-century cross-cultural literature below inherited largely unchanged. Sanskrit aesthetics, including rasa theory, entered European scholarship through the colonial-era philological recovery of Sanskrit literature. And modern cross-cultural psychology — the studies in the next section — is itself an extended case of the Greek-to-constructionist lineage's own instruments meeting populations outside it. In every documented case, the contact ran one direction: a researcher trained inside the European lineage brought a prepared category list outward, not the reverse. A history arguing against treating any one lineage as the default has an obligation to notice when its own sources are shaped by exactly that asymmetry.

## Why "six universal emotions" became consensus anyway

Here is the payoff this whole genealogy has been building toward. If the unit of analysis has never held still — not across five independent traditions, and not even within the European lineage on its own — how did "six universal emotions" come to feel, in the popular imagination, less like one tradition's answer and more like settled biology?

### A design that cannot fail to find agreement

Ekman and Friesen's landmark studies with the Fore of Papua New Guinea (late 1960s–1971) are usually summarized as showing that an isolated culture with no exposure to Western media could correctly match facial photographs to emotions at well-above-chance rates. Worth stating precisely, because the precision is exactly what gets lost in the summary: the task wasn't "look at this face and say what it shows." It was: for each photograph, choose the one word, from a short supplied list — typically the six target emotions — that best fits ([overview of the forced-choice design and its critiques](https://thinkingfeelingbeing.com/2025/08/24/the-science-of-facial-expressions-paul-ekman-universality-and-the-debate-over-emotion/)). A forced-choice design of this kind cannot, by construction, tell you whether a culture independently carves feeling into those six categories — it can only tell you whether, *given* those six as options, people match faces to them better than chance. Even a fairly weak, partial correspondence between a culture's own concepts and the supplied categories clears that bar. By contrast, **free-labelling** designs (describe the face in your own words, no list supplied) and **free-sorting** designs (group a set of faces however seems natural, no predetermined number of groups) don't furnish the categories in advance — and agreement rates on those tasks, even within single Western populations, run substantially lower than forced-choice agreement, a gap the literature had already noted internally well before it became central to later critiques ([an early paper on the forced-choice paradigm](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11195893/)).

### What happens when the categories aren't handed over first

Maria Gendron, working in Lisa Feldman Barrett's lab, spent eighteen days with the Himba of Namibia — a population with minimal exposure to Western media — and ran a free-sorting task: participants sorted photographs of posed expressions into piles, with no constraint on how many piles or what basis to sort by. Rather than six neat emotion-based piles, Himba participants produced varying numbers of groups, the same image sometimes landing in more than one pile across participants, and subsequent analysis found Himba sorting organized substantially around *behavior and situation* rather than the six posited categories — while a parallel free-sort with Boston participants produced much closer-to-consensus groupings ([Gendron et al., "Perceptions of Emotion From Facial Expressions Are Not Culturally Universal," *Emotion*, 2014](https://affective-science.org/pubs/2014/gendron-himba-emotion-2014.pdf); [a plain-language summary of the fieldwork](https://time.com/14478/emotions-may-not-be-so-universal-after-all/)). Gendron and Barrett's own reading, in a later review, is that the apparent universal consensus the forced-choice literature had reported since the 1970s was substantially an artifact of a study design that hands participants the categories before asking whether they use them ([Gendron & Barrett, "Universality Reconsidered," 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0963721417746794)).

### What basic-emotion theory got right

A genuine reckoning has to say this plainly: basic-emotion theory generated an enormous amount of real, useful, falsifiable science, and some of its central claims have held up under methods its own critics designed. Darwin's and Ekman's prediction that *some* facial patterns would be recognized above chance across unrelated cultures has survived later critiques in weakened but real form — a large meta-analysis found above-chance cross-cultural recognition across dozens of studies, even while documenting a robust "in-group advantage" (recognition is more accurate within one's own cultural group than across groups), which Barrett's side reads as evidence *against* strict universality and Ekman's side reads as evidence *for* a universal core modulated by cultural "dialects" ([Elfenbein & Ambady, 2002](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/on-the-universality-and-cultural-specificity-of-emotion-recogniti/)). A study of congenitally blind judo athletes at the 2004 Olympic and Paralympic Games found no measurable difference between blind and sighted athletes' spontaneous facial expressions of victory and defeat — hard to explain if those specific expressions were purely learned by visual observation ([summary of the Matsumoto & Willingham findings](https://blogs.scientificamerican.com/psysociety/blind-athletes-provide-clues-about-the-nature-of-our-emotions/)). And FACS itself, whatever theory it was built to serve, remains a rigorous, theory-agnostic measurement tool that constructionist researchers — Barrett's own group included — still use, because a precise vocabulary for facial movement is valuable independent of which theory of category-formation turns out to be right.

### A live dispute, not a settled one

**This is the honest state of things, and it should be read as a live dispute rather than a verdict this course is handing down.** There's broad agreement that *some* facial configurations are recognized above chance across most tested cultures under most tested conditions, and broad agreement that agreement rates are highly sensitive to method — posed versus spontaneous stimuli, forced-choice versus free response, in-group versus out-group judges. What remains genuinely contested is whether that residual, method-sensitive agreement is best explained by Ekman's discrete, universal affect programs modulated by cultural display rules, or by Barrett's constructed-emotion account, in which a smaller set of genuinely universal ingredients — core affect, and perhaps a handful of coarse valence/arousal-linked signals — get built into culturally specific categories that happen to resemble each other more than chance would predict, without any of them being "basic" in Ekman's sense. Ekman and his collaborators have responded to the free-sort critiques substantively, not by conceding; the exchange is ongoing.

### Why it spread anyway: infrastructure outruns hypothesis

A scientific claim that becomes infrastructure is harder to dislodge than one that stays a journal-article hypothesis, because dislodging it now costs something beyond intellectual consistency. Basic-emotion theory became infrastructure on several fronts at once. **FACS** is still the standard tool for coding facial movement in emotion research, built inside a program that assumes a small set of discrete target emotions to code *for* ([Ekman's own history of FACS](https://paulekman.medium.com/the-history-of-the-facial-action-coding-system-facs-8787a86ed81f)). The U.S. Transportation Security Administration's **SPOT program**, deployed from 2007, trained roughly 3,000 officers on microexpression-based indicators drawing on Ekman's work and spent an estimated $900 million in its first six years; a 2013 U.S. Government Accountability Office report found no scientifically validated evidence the behavioral indicators could reliably identify security threats ([the TSA program's Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BDA_(TSA_program)); [Paul Ekman Group's own response to the GAO finding](https://www.paulekman.com/blog/ineffectively-testing-the-effectiveness-of-tsa/)). A large **affective-computing** industry — marketing analytics, hiring-screen tools, driver-monitoring systems — infers emotional state from facial video largely on the assumption that a small set of discrete, visually legible categories exist to be detected; the ACLU and multiple researchers have argued this industry runs ahead of the science it claims to rest on ([ACLU, "Experts Say 'Emotion Recognition' Lacks Scientific Foundation"](https://www.aclu.org/news/privacy-technology/experts-say-emotion-recognition-lacks-scientific)). And **popular culture** did the rest: Paul Ekman consulted directly on Pixar's *Inside Out* (2015), whose five character-emotions are explicitly drawn from his research program and have since become, for a generation of children and the parents who watched with them, the default folk taxonomy of feeling ([background on Ekman's consulting role](https://www.paulekman.com/projects/inside-out/)).

None of this is evidence for or against the theory. That's the point: infrastructure and popularity are orthogonal to truth, but they're not orthogonal to how hard a theory is to revise once it's load-bearing for a $900-million security program and a movie every literate parent in the anglophone world has shown their child. "Six universal emotions" became consensus because a forced-choice methodology that couldn't help but manufacture the appearance of cross-cultural agreement was applied, with genuine rigor and good faith, across hundreds of studies before its weaker alternatives were tried seriously at scale — and because, once the theory had a precise measurement tool, a security-policy application, and a beloved animated film behind it, the cost of revising it stopped being purely intellectual.

## The throughline

Step back across all five lineages and one pattern holds where nothing else does: the unit of analysis itself keeps changing. Aquinas sorted eleven passions by arduousness, not pleasantness. Bharata's *rasa* is an event between performer and audience, not a private inner state. Descartes counted six primitive passions in 1649 — three centuries before Ekman's six, and not the same six. Chinese medicine correlates seven emotions with five viscera. Amazonian perspectivism refuses to produce a list at all. And "emotion" itself — the very word doing the organizing in most of this course — is a nineteenth-century English coinage that displaced passions, affections, and sentiments, not a timeless category modern science finally got around to measuring correctly.

None of that means every account is equally supported, or that nothing here is more carefully tested than anything else — which is exactly why every item in this library carries an honest `evidence_tier`, and why a map being *constructed* is not the same as a map being *wrong*. It means the categories were never simply *there*, waiting. They were built, by particular people, answering particular questions, in particular centuries — which is what makes it possible, and legitimate, for a person today to pick up a school of feeling that fits, use it well, and know exactly what kind of thing they're holding.
