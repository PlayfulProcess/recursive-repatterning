# Sleep, gently: a short course

This course walks through the practice [Sleep, gently](../deck.html?file=schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json) one idea at a time. Each lesson is short. Each ends with one practice step you can try tonight or tomorrow morning.

It is psychoeducation, not therapy or treatment. It explains what cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) teaches, and what the research says about a few common questions, so you can understand your own nights and talk about them with a clinician. It does not diagnose anything and it does not replace care.

> If the night brings thoughts of harming yourself, that is not a sleep problem. Call a crisis line or emergency services now.

## Is broken sleep a problem?

Waking in the night is part of ordinary sleep. In sleep-lab studies of healthy adults, some time awake after first falling asleep is normal, and it tends to grow with age ([Ohayon et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/27.7.1255), a meta-analysis of 65 studies and 3,577 people).

Insomnia, as a disorder, asks for more than broken nights. The two main diagnostic manuals, DSM-5 and ICSD-3, set out the same shape. There is trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early, even with a fair chance to sleep. It brings distress or a cost in the day: tiredness, poor focus, low mood, worry about sleep. And it happens at least three nights a week, for at least three months. Both sets of criteria are printed side by side in a free table in [Brewster, Riegel & Gehrman 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsmc.2017.09.002) (free: [PMC5847293](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5847293/)).

So a waking you rest through, followed by a day that goes fine, is not a disorder. A pattern that costs you in the day deserves attention, even before the three months are up.

**Practice.** Each morning this week, ask one question: did last night cost me anything today? Write yes or no on the last line of the [morning diary](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=morning-diary), under "Anything else".

## First sleep and second sleep

The historian A. Roger Ekirch found that English writing from before electric light often speaks of a "first sleep" and a "second sleep", with a waking interval between them. He drew on diaries, court records and medical writing, among other sources, and argued that two sleeps a night were common before the industrial age ([Ekirch 2001](https://doi.org/10.1086/ahr/106.2.343)).

A laboratory study points the same way. When healthy volunteers moved from a 16-hour day to a 10-hour day, with long dark nights, their sleep often split into two bouts with one to three hours awake between them ([Wehr 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.1992.tb00019.x)).

The idea has critics. A study of three present-day pre-industrial societies measured sleep that was not broken by long periods of waking ([Yetish et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.09.046)); Ekirch replied ([Ekirch 2016](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.5558)). A study of a farming community in Madagascar without electricity found a burst of activity after midnight on about half of the nights, which its authors read as segmented sleep ([Samson et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajhb.22979)). A historian re-read the early modern texts and argued that they allow other readings too ([Boyce 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2023.14), open access); Ekirch answered ([Ekirch 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2024.20)), and Boyce responded ([Boyce 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2024.18)). The question is open.

What it suggests for one person is modest. People can sleep in one block or in two, depending on light, long nights and custom. A calm hour awake in the middle of the night is not, by itself, a sign that something is broken. What matters is the same as in the first lesson: how the day goes, and whether the waking brings distress.

**Practice.** If you wake tonight, try calling it "the interval" instead of "insomnia". Keep it dim, dull and calm. Go back to bed when sleepy (see [What to do at 3am](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=three-am)).

## Four hours and feeling great?

A fair question: if someone sleeps four hours and feels great, is that fine?

Sometimes it is. Natural short sleepers exist. Researchers in the Fu and Ptáček lab describe them as people with a life-long tendency to sleep only about four to six hours a night and feel well rested ([Shi et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026)). In families with this trait the lab has found rare gene variants: in DEC2, where carriers in one family averaged 6.25 hours of sleep against 8.06 hours for relatives without the variant ([He et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1174443)); in ADRB1 ([Shi et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026)); and in NPSR1 ([Xing et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.aax2014)). These are rare. The studies describe a life-long trait that runs in families, not a habit someone trained into. For most adults, sleep societies recommend seven or more hours a night ([Watson et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716)).

There is a catch in "feeling great". In a laboratory study, healthy adults spent 14 days at four, six or eight hours in bed a night. At four and six hours, performance on every cognitive task got steadily worse. Sleepiness ratings rose at first, then barely moved, and did not tell the six-hour group from the four-hour group. After two weeks at six hours or less, the losses matched up to two nights with no sleep at all. The authors read the sleepiness ratings as a sign that the participants were largely unaware of their growing deficits ([Van Dongen et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117)).

So feeling great is good evidence, but not complete evidence. What to watch is how the day works, and whether it holds over weeks: nodding off when sitting still, reading or driving; needing caffeine to get through; mistakes or a short temper that others notice; sleeping much longer whenever you get the chance.

One different case needs a clinician, not a test. If you suddenly need much less sleep than usual, with much more energy or racing thoughts, in a way that is not like you, call your clinician.

**Practice.** For two weeks, add one daytime line to the diary under "Anything else": did I nod off, or nearly, while sitting still? Ask someone close how you have seemed. If you nod off while driving or sitting, see a doctor ([When to see a doctor](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=see-a-doctor)).

## Two forces: sleep drive and the body clock

Two forces decide when we sleep. Sleep drive builds the longer we are awake, like sand in an hourglass, and sleep empties it. A nap or a long lie-in spends some of it early, so less is left for the night. The body clock runs on a cycle of about a day, and light sets it, morning light most of all.

We sleep best when the two line up: plenty of sleep drive, at the hour the clock calls night. This two-process model, first proposed by Alexander Borbély in 1982, is still the standard frame ([Borbély et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12371)). Most of the behavioural part of CBT-I works on these two forces.

**Practice.** Notice today what spends your sleep drive early: a nap, a lie-in, a doze on the sofa. Just notice. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=two-processes).

## One wake time

Pick one time to get up, and keep it every day, weekends too. It anchors the body clock and starts the hourglass of sleep drive. After a bad night the urge is to sleep in. Getting up anyway is what makes the next night easier.

This one change is safe for almost everyone, and it is often the place to start. In a case series of people with bipolar disorder and insomnia, regular bed and rise times alone brought marked improvement for most participants ([Kaplan & Harvey 2013](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.12050708)).

**Practice.** Choose a wake time you can keep seven days a week. Set one alarm. Get out of bed when it rings, and go towards light: open the curtains, step outside if you can. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=anchor-wake-time).

## The bed is for sleep

After many hours of lying awake, the bed itself can start to mean "awake and worried". Stimulus control teaches the body again that bed means sleep. It was introduced by Richard Bootzin in 1972, and the 2021 American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline gives it a conditional recommendation as a single therapy ([Edinger et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986)).

Go to bed when sleepy, not just tired, and not before your chosen bedtime. Use the bed for sleep and sex only. If you are awake for what feels like about 20 minutes, get up, go somewhere dim and calm, and come back when sleepy. Guess the time; do not watch the clock. No naps, or one short early-afternoon nap if you need one to be safe.

Some people find getting up at night too stimulating. A clinician can adapt this step, for example by resting in bed with a relaxation practice instead ([Kaplan & Harvey 2013](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.12050708)).

**Practice.** Tonight, take one waking activity out of bed: the phone, the reading, or the worrying. Do it in a chair. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=stimulus-control).

## The sleep window, with care

Many people with insomnia spend far longer in bed than they sleep. The sleep window brings time in bed closer to the sleep you are actually getting, for a while, so sleep drive builds and sleep becomes deeper. Then the window widens again, step by step. Sleep restriction sets it all at once. Sleep compression, tested in older adults ([Lichstein et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.69.2.227)), narrows it slowly; in this practice, by 15 to 30 minutes a week. In the first weeks people are sleepier and slower, returning to baseline by three months ([Kyle et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.3386)).

Talk to a doctor or a trained CBT-I clinician before you shorten your time in bed. Please do not try it on your own if any of these apply:

- bipolar disorder, or a past episode of mania or hypomania (less sleep can tip mood upward);
- epilepsy or any seizures (sleep loss can lower the seizure threshold);
- schizophrenia or another psychotic illness;
- sleep apnoea, or loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing, or another sleep disorder such as restless legs;
- pregnancy;
- you drive for work, fly, operate machinery, work in emergency care, or work shifts;
- you already feel very sleepy in the day, or you already sleep under about five and a half hours.

Stop and call your clinician if you nod off while sitting or driving, or if your mood lifts in a way that is not like you: needing less sleep, racing thoughts, much more energy.

In this practice the window never goes under six hours in bed. Clinical programmes sometimes go lower; that is for supervised care. For self-guided use, compression is the gentler choice, and only after talking with a clinician. These cautions follow the Australian joint position statement on insomnia ([Sweetman et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpag086)), Kaplan & Harvey (2013), and the exclusion lists of sleep restriction trials ([Fleming et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0305304)). Exclusion from trials means little is known, not that harm is proven; it is still a reason to ask first.

**Practice.** Change nothing yet. Keep the diary and the one wake time for two weeks, then bring the numbers to a clinician if a window seems worth trying. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=sleep-window).

## Lowering the alarm

Sleep is not a switch. The body needs a slope down into it. Three practices lower the alarm before bed.

The buffer zone is the last hour or so before bed, protected from work, hard conversations and problem-solving ([NHS, Insomnia](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/)). Worry time moves the thinking to the early evening and onto paper: in a controlled study, writing possible solutions to worries in the early evening lowered pre-sleep mental arousal ([Carney & Waters 2006](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15402010bsm0401_2)). The body scan moves attention slowly through the body without fixing anything; relaxation therapy carries a conditional recommendation in the AASM guideline ([Edinger et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986)).

Practise the body scan in the day first, when the stakes are low. At night, do it for its own sake. If it becomes another way of trying hard to sleep, it stops working.

**Practice.** Early this evening, ten minutes at a table: two columns, "what is on my mind" and "the next small step, or nothing to do tonight". Close the notebook. [Worry time](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=constructive-worry) · [Buffer zone](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=buffer-zone) · [Body scan](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=relaxation-body-scan).

## Checking the facts on sleep thoughts

Lying awake, the mind makes predictions. "If I don't sleep now, tomorrow is ruined." "I'll never sleep normally again." These thoughts raise the alarm, the alarm keeps us awake, and the night seems to prove the thought. The cognitive model of insomnia describes this loop of worry, watching for threat and mistaken beliefs about sleep ([Harvey 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-7967(01)00061-4)).

"I will never sleep" feels like a fact about the body. It is a prediction, built from past nights and present fear, and predictions can be tested.

**Practice.** In the day, not at 3am, take one sleep thought. What actually happened after my last bad night? What else fits the same facts? Write a kinder, truer sentence beside it, such as: "A poor night makes tomorrow harder. I have done hard days before." [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=sleep-thoughts).

## Light, caffeine and alcohol

Morning daylight tells the body clock that day has begun, and bright light late in the evening pushes it later ([Blume et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11818-019-00215-x)). Caffeine blocks the feeling of sleep drive and lasts: 400 mg taken even six hours before bed disturbed sleep compared with placebo ([Drake et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170)). Alcohol can shorten the time to fall asleep, then breaks up sleep in the second half of the night ([Ebrahim et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006)).

These facts help, but on their own they are rarely enough for chronic insomnia; most people need more than basic sleep hygiene ([AASM patient guide](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Behavioral-and-Psychological-Treatments-for-Insomnia-Patient-Guide.pdf)).

**Practice.** Get outside light within an hour of waking. Keep caffeine to the morning, and note caffeine and alcohol in the diary so you can see your own pattern. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=light-caffeine-alcohol).

## Cannabis and sleep: what the evidence says

Many people use cannabis in the evening, and many say it helps them sleep. Here is what the research shows, and how strong it is. This lesson does not advise starting, stopping or changing cannabis or any medicine. Any change belongs in a conversation with your clinician, especially if the cannabis is prescribed or you take other medicines.

**Falling asleep.** In the short term, THC (the main intoxicating compound) may shorten the time it takes to fall asleep ([Babson, Sottile & Morabito 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-017-0775-9)). Acute use also appears to increase deep, slow-wave sleep ([Schierenbeck et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2007.12.004)).

**Daily use and tolerance.** With chronic use, people develop tolerance to most of these effects, including the sleep-inducing effect and the extra slow-wave sleep. Studies find sleep efficiency in daily users unimproved or worse ([Angarita et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13722-016-0056-7), free). A 2017 review summarised it this way: THC may help with falling asleep but could impair sleep quality over the long term ([Babson et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-017-0775-9)).

**REM sleep and dreams.** Smoked cannabis and oral THC reduce REM sleep, the stage most linked with vivid dreaming ([Schierenbeck et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2007.12.004)). Studies of daily users disagree about REM time, reporting less, no change, or more ([Angarita et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13722-016-0056-7)).

**Stopping after regular use.** Difficulty sleeping and strange dreams are among the most consistently reported symptoms of cannabis withdrawal, with longer time to fall asleep, less slow-wave sleep and a rebound of REM sleep ([Schierenbeck et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2007.12.004)). In an outpatient study of 18 users, withdrawal effects, sleep problems among them, typically began one to three days after stopping, peaked at two to six days, and mostly lasted four to fourteen days ([Budney et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843x.112.3.393)); strange dreams have been reported lasting as long as 45 days ([Angarita et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13722-016-0056-7)). Sleep-lab recordings agree. Seventeen heavy users who had just stopped slept less and had less slow-wave sleep than 14 non-users, and on the second night took longer to fall asleep, with worse sleep efficiency ([Bolla et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/31.6.901)). Twenty daily users who paused for three days slept less efficiently, took longer to fall asleep and spent more time in REM sleep than when they were using ([Vandrey et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2011.01.003)). The Bolla study could not tell how much of the difference was there before stopping.

**Medical use.** A 2017 National Academies report found moderate evidence that cannabinoids, mainly nabiximols (a THC and CBD spray), improve short-term sleep outcomes in people whose sleep is disturbed by sleep apnoea, fibromyalgia, chronic pain or multiple sclerosis ([National Academies 2017](https://doi.org/10.17226/24625); [AASM summary](https://aasm.org/new-report-by-the-national-academies-examines-effects-of-cannabis-on-sleep/)).

**How strong is the evidence?** Not strong yet. A systematic review of 39 publications found mixed results and methodological problems that preclude any definitive conclusion ([Gates, Albertella & Copeland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2014.02.005)). A critical review of clinical trials found that most studied people with a chronic illness, measured sleep as a secondary outcome, had small samples, and seldom used validated sleep measures; it called for more work on how THC and CBD differ ([Kuhathasan et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/pha0000285)). One review calls the research "in its infancy" ([Babson et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-017-0775-9)), and long-term effects remain an open question ([Kuhathasan et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/pha0000285)).

**Practice.** If cannabis is part of your evenings, note it in the diary under "Anything else", the way you note caffeine and alcohol: what, roughly how much, and the last time. After a few weeks you will see your own pattern, and you can take it to your clinician.

## What to do at 3am

What turns a waking into an hour awake is usually the fight: checking the clock, counting the hours left, trying hard to sleep. Clock-watching is one of the processes the cognitive model says keeps insomnia going ([Harvey 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-7967(01)00061-4)).

Two errors pull at 3am: catastrophizing (tomorrow is ruined) and fighting (I must sleep now). The middle is acceptance and change together: accept this waking, and keep the plan that changes the pattern.

If the night brings thoughts of harming yourself, that is not a sleep problem. Call a crisis line or emergency services now.

**Practice.** Turn the clock away. Let the body rest; rest is not nothing. If you feel restless, get up, keep the light dim and do something calm and dull, and go back when sleepy. Say the evening line to yourself: I accept tonight as it comes. I keep my wake time. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=three-am).

## When to see a doctor

Insomnia is common and treatable, and CBT-I is the first recommended treatment for chronic insomnia in adults ([Edinger et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986); [Qaseem et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7326/M15-2175)). Some sleep problems need a doctor, not a practice.

See a doctor if:

- changing your sleep habits has not helped;
- you have had trouble sleeping for months;
- it is making it hard to cope with daily life;
- someone hears you snore loudly, gasp, or stop breathing in your sleep, or you wake with a dry mouth or a headache;
- you feel very sleepy in the day, or nod off while driving;
- you have an urge to move your legs at night, pain, night sweats, or another health change;
- your mood is low or high for days, or anxiety is heavy;
- you want to change or stop a sleep medicine. Do not stop one suddenly on your own.

Get help now if you have thoughts of harming yourself: call a crisis line or emergency services.

These points follow [NHS, Insomnia](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/) and [NHLBI, Sleep apnea symptoms](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea/symptoms). Sleep apnoea and other sleep disorders can look like insomnia and may need a sleep study first ([Sweetman et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpag086)).

**Practice.** Read the list once and tick anything that fits. If something does, book the appointment before starting any sleep window, and take your diary. [Open the item](../viewers/cards.html?src=../schools/sleep-gently/grammar.json&item=see-a-doctor).

## The daily practice

The practice has three parts. In the evening, ten minutes of worry time, then an hour of slope, then one line in your private journal: *Tonight I accept ______. Tomorrow I keep ______.* In the morning, two minutes of diary within an hour of getting up, with guesses, never clock-watching in the night. Once a week, four numbers from the diary (sleep efficiency, minutes to fall asleep, minutes awake in the night, and a 1 to 5 for how rested the week felt) and one decision at most.

The diary fields follow the Consensus Sleep Diary ([Carney et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.1642)). Your answers live only in your own private journal, never in this library.

The diary is a set of rough guesses, and that is fine. Some people chase perfect sleep data until the chase itself keeps them awake ([Baron et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.6472)). If tracking makes nights more anxious, pause it for a week. The aim is rest, not a perfect score.

**Practice.** Tonight, write the evening line. Tomorrow morning, copy the blank diary into your private journal and fill it in.
