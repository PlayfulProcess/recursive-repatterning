# Fact-check: "unpublished training + exclusion" claim about cross-cultural emotion research

**Prepared for:** PlayfulProcess
**Status:** research complete, verdict below
**Scope:** verify a specific recollected claim from Lisa Feldman Barrett's *How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain* (2017) against primary sources and peer-reviewed critique.

---

## 1. The claim as stated

This is PlayfulProcess's recollection, not a verified fact. As given:

> Barrett's book reports that in cross-cultural emotion research, the researchers did not publish a step in which participants were deliberately taught English emotion words before the study, and participants who failed to "learn" the words were excluded from the sample.

Two readings were checked:

- **(a)** Barrett describing **Paul Ekman's team's** cross-cultural studies with the Fore of Papua New Guinea (c. 1969–1971).
- **(b)** Barrett describing **her own lab's** methodology.

**Neither reading, as stated, was found in citable form.** No source — Barrett's book, her online endnotes, the primary Ekman literature, or the peer-reviewed critical literature (Russell 1994, Gendron/Barrett papers, Leys 2017, Crivelli & Fridlund) — documents an *unpublished* step in which participants were taught English emotion words and then *excluded from the sample* for failing to learn them.

What **is** documented, and is almost certainly the memory's real source, is a different, adjacent, and less severe methodological issue, detailed in Section 4. It involves Ekman, but not the classic Fore studies, and it was published, not hidden.

---

## 2. What is documented and citable

### 2.1 Barrett's own endnotes (the book's actual sourcing)

*How Emotions Are Made* has extensive endnotes, both in print and extended online at how-emotions-are-made.com. I obtained and read the full printed endnotes PDF (`how-emotions-are-made.com/w/images/heam/5/57/Notes.pdf`). The relevant chapter is **Chapter 3, "The Myth of Universal Emotions."**

Chapter 3, note 16 (referencing a study Barrett is critiquing):

> "in the story was feeling: 'Each participant was asked, after each story, how the target person was feeling, in order to ensure that the participant had understood the story correctly' (Sauter et al. 2015, 355). Sauter et al. have called this extra step a 'manipulation check'... 'in their own words': Sauter et al. 2015, 355 (emphasis added)."

Chapter 3, note 17:

> "Himba participants had to 'explain the intended emotion in their own words, before they proceeded to the experimental trials for that story' (Sauter et al. 2015, 355). That is, all the trials were delivered one right after the other, in what scientists call a 'block' of trials."

This is the passage the maintainer's memory most plausibly compresses. It is about a **published methodological step** (a "manipulation check") in a study **co-authored by Paul Ekman** — but it is not the 1969/1971 Fore work, and it does not describe teaching English words or excluding non-learners.

### 2.2 The study in question: Sauter, Eisner, Ekman & Scott

Two papers, same research program, both co-authored by Paul Ekman, testing recognition of **nonverbal emotional vocalizations** (not facial expressions) among the **Himba of northern Namibia** (not the Fore of Papua New Guinea):

- Sauter, D. A., Eisner, F., Ekman, P., & Scott, S. K. (2010). "Cross-cultural recognition of basic emotions through nonverbal emotional vocalizations." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 107(6), 2408–2412.
- Sauter, D. A., Eisner, F., Ekman, P., & Scott, S. K. (2015). "Emotional Vocalizations Are Recognized Across Cultures Regardless of the Valence of Distractors." *Psychological Science*, 26(3), 354–356.

Method, quoted directly from the 2015 paper (as reproduced in Barrett's own endnote, cited above): participants heard a story, and could **"listen several times to a given recorded story (if needed), until they could explain the intended emotion in their own words, before they proceeded to the experimental trials for that story."**

This is a real, published comprehension check — repeated listening until the participant could restate the story's emotional content, *before* the forced-choice matching trials for that emotion began.

A correction notice was later issued: Sauter, Eisner, Ekman & Scott, "Correction for... Cross-cultural recognition of basic emotions through nonverbal emotional vocalizations," *PNAS* 112(23): E3086 (2015). It clarified that trials were **blocked by emotion** — i.e., the comprehension-check-then-trial sequence for a given emotion happened as a unit, all trials for that emotion back-to-back, rather than emotions being interleaved. This confirms the mechanism Barrett flags in note 17.

### 2.3 The peer-reviewed critique of that step

Gendron, M., Roberson, D., & Barrett, L. F. (2015). "Cultural variation in emotion perception is real: A response to Sauter et al." *Psychological Science*, 26(3), 357–359.

Direct quote:

> "The 'rigorous' manipulation check that Sauter et al. now describe likely encouraged category learning for Himba participants before they were allowed to complete experimental trials."

This is the actual, citable, peer-reviewed claim closest to what the maintainer recalls: **a published pre-trial step that critics argue functioned as inadvertent category/word-concept training**, potentially inflating the appearance of cross-cultural agreement. It was made by Barrett's own co-authors (Gendron, Roberson) with Barrett as a co-author of the rebuttal.

**This response contains no claim that participants were excluded from the sample.** It criticizes the procedure as a confound in the *published* study, not as something hidden or as a basis for dropping non-learners.

### 2.4 The classic Ekman/Fore studies (1969, 1971) — what their methods actually say

- Ekman, P., Sorenson, E. R., & Friesen, W. V. (1969). "Pan-Cultural Elements in Facial Displays of Emotion." *Science*, 164(3875), 86–88.
- Ekman, P., & Friesen, W. V. (1971). "Constants across cultures in the face and emotion." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 17(2), 124–129.

Because most Fore participants were non-literate and it was unclear whether Fore had single-word equivalents for the relevant English emotion terms, Ekman's team did **not** use an English-word list with the Fore. Instead they used the "Dashiell method" (after psychologist John Dashiell, 1927): participants were told a short story tuned to a target emotion and asked to select the matching face from a small set of photographs — this is confirmed in Barrett's own Chapter 1 endnote 6 ("face best matches the story: E.g., Ekman and Friesen 1971. This is called the 'Dashiell' method").

The 1969 Fore sample was explicitly **screened for isolation from Western contact** at recruitment — a published, non-secret selection criterion, not a post-hoc exclusion for failing a word-learning test: researchers sought Fore participants who had never seen a film, could not speak or read English or Pidgin, and had not lived in a Western settlement or worked for a Westerner. That is a genuine, documented "who's in the sample" decision — but it screens for cultural isolation, not for failure to learn emotion words, and it appears in the published method, not as an unpublished step.

I could not locate, in what was accessible, any passage in Ekman, Sorenson & Friesen (1969) or Ekman & Friesen (1971) describing participants being taught English emotion words and dropped from the sample for failing to learn them.

### 2.5 The critical literature (Russell 1994 and after)

Russell, J. A. (1994). "Is there universal recognition of emotion from facial expression? A review of the cross-cultural studies." *Psychological Bulletin*, 115(1), 102–141.

This is the review Barrett herself cites repeatedly in Chapter 3 (nine separate endnote references) as the authoritative catalogue of methodological problems with the Ekman-paradigm literature: forced-choice response format, within-subject design, preselected photographs, and translator-mediated administration. I was able to confirm, via a direct quote pulled from the paper, Russell's general point that forced-choice recognition studies show "people spontaneously recognize the predicted emotion" less than the paradigm assumes, once the forced-choice scaffolding is loosened. I could **not** obtain the full text of Russell (1994) to search exhaustively for a specific "taught words, excluded non-learners" claim, and no secondary source describing Russell's critique that I found mentions such a claim. This is a genuine gap in what I could verify — flagged rather than assumed away.

I found no relevant claim of this kind in the material I could access from Gendron, Crivelli & Barrett's "Universality Reconsidered" (*Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 2018) or Crivelli & Fridlund's Trobriand Islands work, beyond the general (and separately well-documented) point that constrained matching tasks can shape or inflate agreement scores. I did not have direct access to Ruth Leys, *The Ascent of Affect* (2017), and could not verify or rule out a mention there; this should be treated as unchecked, not as negative evidence.

## 3. What is NOT documented

Being explicit, since this is the point of the exercise:

1. **"Did not publish"** — false, as applied to the closest matching study (Sauter/Eisner/Ekman/Scott). The comprehension-check step is described in the published method section, quoted with a page number by Barrett herself (2015, p. 355), and further clarified in a 2015 published correction.
2. **"Excluded from the sample"** — not found anywhere in the sources I could access: not in Sauter et al. (2010, 2015), not in the Gendron/Roberson/Barrett (2015) critique of that study, not in Ekman, Sorenson & Friesen (1969), and not in Ekman & Friesen (1971). No source states that participants who failed a word-learning or comprehension step were dropped from the reported sample.
3. **Applied specifically to the classic Fore studies (reading a)** — not substantiated. Those studies used story/vignette matching (the Dashiell method) precisely *because* English-word instruction wasn't viable with non-literate Fore participants; the only documented sample-selection criterion is a published Westernization/contact screen, not a word-learning test.
4. **Applied to Barrett's own lab (reading b)** — not substantiated, and if anything the opposite is closer to true: Barrett's lab studies with the Himba (Gendron et al. 2014b, described in Chapter 3 endnotes) are presented as deliberately *avoiding* word-based, forced-choice methods (free sorting of faces, and — separately — a version providing emotion words as a comparison condition). Barrett/Gendron are the *critics* of the training-like manipulation check, not its authors.

## 4. Nearest verified relative

The best-supported account of what the maintainer is likely remembering:

**A real, published, peer-reviewed methodological dispute exists — but it is milder than recalled, involves a different researcher pairing, a different population, and a different mechanism:**

- **Who:** Disa Sauter, Frank Eisner, Sophie Scott, and **Paul Ekman as a co-author** (not Ekman leading a solo team) — a 2010/2015 vocal-emotion-recognition study, not the classic 1969/1971 Fore facial-expression studies.
- **Where:** the **Himba of northern Namibia**, not the Fore of Papua New Guinea.
- **What:** a "manipulation check" — participants re-heard a story until they could restate its emotional content in their own words — run immediately before the forced-choice trials for that same emotion, with all trials for an emotion delivered as a block.
- **Publication status:** described in the papers' own method sections, quoted by Barrett with an exact page citation, and further clarified (not concealed) in a subsequent published correction.
- **The critique, as actually published** (Gendron, Roberson & Barrett 2015): the "rigorous" comprehension check **"likely encouraged category learning... before [participants] were allowed to complete experimental trials"** — i.e., a *confound that may have functioned like informal training*, not an admission of secrecy, and not paired with any claim about excluding people from the sample.

A second, weaker adjacent thread, also real and published: the original 1969 Fore sample was deliberately screened for isolation from Western media/contact (no film exposure, no English/Pidgin, no Western employment). That is a genuine, disclosed sample-selection criterion — but it screens for cultural contact, not for emotion-word competence, and there is no record of it functioning as an exclusion-after-training step.

---

## 5. Verdict

**Unverified — could not find**, for the claim as stated in either reading (a) or (b).

**The nearest documented relative is real and citable**: a published pre-trial "manipulation check" in the Sauter/Eisner/Ekman/Scott Himba vocalization studies (2010, 2015), which Barrett's own co-authors (Gendron & Roberson, with Barrett) publicly argued in *Psychological Science* likely taught emotion categories to participants before they were tested on them. That critique is solid and directly citable. The "unpublished" and "excluded from the sample" elements of the recollection are not supported by anything found.

If sources conflict on anything here, it is only on emphasis (Sauter et al. treat the manipulation check as a legitimate comprehension safeguard; Gendron/Roberson/Barrett treat it as a likely confound) — not on the underlying facts of what the check was or that it was published.

## 6. How the library should phrase it, if at all

**Defensible wording**, if PlayfulProcess wants to reference this in something public-facing:

> In *How Emotions Are Made*, Lisa Feldman Barrett (with co-authors Maria Gendron and Debi Roberson) argued in a 2015 *Psychological Science* reply that a "manipulation check" used in a cross-cultural vocal-emotion study with the Himba of Namibia — co-authored by Paul Ekman — may have functioned as inadvertent training in Western emotion categories before participants were tested (Gendron, Roberson, & Barrett, 2015, p. 358). The step itself was published in the original study's methods, not concealed, and no participants are documented as having been excluded for failing it.

**Not defensible, and should not be used:**

> Ekman's researchers secretly taught the Fore of Papua New Guinea English emotion words and threw out anyone who couldn't learn them.

That sentence is not supported by any source located in this research and should not be attributed to Barrett, to Ekman's original Fore studies, or to the peer-reviewed record.
