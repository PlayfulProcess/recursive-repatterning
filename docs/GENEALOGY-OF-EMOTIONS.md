## Why a genealogy, and not a survey

Every school catalogued in this library — Plutchik's wheel, Nonviolent Communication's
needs, Gottman's and Johnson's couples work, DBT's skills, Jungian parts — presents its
categories as if they were simply *there*, waiting to be named. This document exists to
show that they are not. The unit of analysis itself — what counts as one feeling, how
many there are, what axis organizes them, whether the container "emotion" is even the
right container — has been rebuilt from scratch by every tradition that has taken up the
problem of feeling, ancient to contemporary. That is not a scandal. It is the reason this
library can hold Aristotle, Bharata, Aquinas, Descartes, Darwin, Ekman, and Barrett
side by side without asking any of them to be secretly agreeing with each other.

The library's own premise, stated in the [README](../README.md), is that the
constructionist account is the *substrate* — not because it is the last word, but
because "no fixed biological fingerprint" is where the historical record, read honestly,
actually points. This document is the evidence for that reading: a history of the
categories changing, and an account of why one particular set of categories — "six basic
emotions" — got mistaken for a discovery rather than recognized as one interface among
many.

## A compact timeline

| Era | Tradition / figure | Unit of analysis | Organizing axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. 4th c. BCE | Aristotle, *Rhetoric* | *pathē* — situational, rhetoric-bound | Persuasive/judgment-altering effect, not a fixed list |
| 3rd c. BCE onward | Stoics (Chrysippus et al.) | Four *pathē* genera + three *eupatheiai* | Correct vs. mistaken value-judgment about present/future good/bad |
| 200 BCE – 200 CE | Bharata, *Nāṭyaśāstra* | 8 *rasas* (a 9th, *śānta*, added by Abhinavagupta, 11th c.) | Aesthetic "flavor" produced in an audience, not a felt state in an individual |
| Han dynasty onward | Chinese classical medicine | *Qiqing*, the seven emotions | Correspondence to the five viscera (zang) and qi dynamics |
| 13th c. | Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* | 11 passions (6 concupiscible + 5 irascible) | Ease vs. arduousness of the object, **not** pleasant/unpleasant |
| 1649 | Descartes, *Les passions de l'âme* | 6 "primitive" passions | Causal primitives from which all others compound |
| 1677 | Spinoza, *Ethics* | 3 primary affects | Transitions in the body's power of acting |
| 1739 | Hume, *Treatise* | Direct vs. indirect passions | Object of the passion (self/other) and causal complexity |
| 19th c. (per Dixon 2003) | English psychology | "Emotions" as a new secular category | Displaces passions/affections/sentiments/appetites |
| 1872 | Darwin | Universal expressions | Evolutionary continuity, humans and animals |
| 1884 | William James | Bodily perception *is* the emotion | Somatic feedback, not cognitive judgment |
| 1962 | Tomkins | 9 innate affects | Hard-wired subcortical programs |
| 1970s–90s | Ekman, Plutchik | 6 (Ekman) / 8 (Plutchik) basic emotions | Discrete, universal, biologically "basic" categories |
| 1980 | Russell | 2D circumplex (valence × arousal) | Continuous dimensions, not discrete categories |
| 2000s–present | Barrett | Constructed emotion | Core affect + concept, built in the moment |
| 2017 | Leys | Historiographic critique | The field's own recurring intentionality-vs-universality split |

## Part A — The genealogy: how the unit of analysis kept changing

### Ancient Greece: *pathē* as rhetorical, not clinical

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (Book II) does not offer a taxonomy of emotion in anything like
the modern sense. It offers a handbook for orators: a *pathos* is "that on account of
which people change and differ in their judgments, and which is accompanied by pain and
pleasure" — anger, pity, fear, and their opposites are analyzed one by one, each defined
by the situation that provokes it, the state of mind of the person it targets, and the
grounds on which it can be talked up or talked down ([Stanford Encyclopedia of
Philosophy, "Ancient, Medieval and Renaissance Theories of the
Emotions"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2014/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD1Background.html);
[overview of Aristotle's treatment of the *pathē*](https://sites.dwrl.utexas.edu/davis/courses/rhe-330e-pathos/197-2/aristotle-on-the-pathe/)).
Notably, the *Nicomachean Ethics* gives a broader list — appetite, anger, fear,
confidence, envy, joy, love, hatred, longing, emulation, pity — but the *Rhetoric*
narrows it, dropping bodily appetite because appetite does not, in Aristotle's framing,
change a judgment. **Established:** there is no single canonical Aristotelian list of
emotions; the count and membership shift with the purpose of the text. That instability
is itself the first data point in this genealogy — the "unit" was never fixed even within
one philosopher's corpus.

The Stoics rebuilt the whole apparatus around a different question: not *what moves an
audience* but *what a mistaken judgment does to the soul*. Chrysippus's *On Passions*
groups the *pathē* into four genera — distress (*lupē*), fear (*phobos*), appetite
(*epithumia*), and pleasure (*hēdonē*) — each one an "irrational and unnatural movement
of the soul" produced by assenting to a false impression about what is good or bad, and
about whether it is present or future
([Wikipedia, "Stoic passions"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoic_passions); [summary of
Chrysippus's *On Passions*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Passions)). Crucially, the
Stoic sage was not meant to feel nothing: alongside the four passions sit three
*eupatheiai*, "good feelings" produced by *correct* judgments — joy (*chara*), wish
(*boulēsis*), and caution (*eulabeia*) — each the rational counterpart of one of the
irrational passions (there is no rational counterpart to pleasure, since for the Stoics
present goods do not licitly produce elation)
([viastoica.com on *eupatheia*](https://viastoica.com/what-is-eupatheia/)). The organizing
axis here is neither valence nor a body-part inventory — it is epistemic: is the
underlying judgment true or false, and is its object present or future. A feeling and its
rational counterpart could be, in modern terms, "the same emotion" and still fall on
opposite sides of the Stoic ledger, because the ledger isn't tracking the feeling — it's
tracking the judgment behind it.

### Ancient India: *rasa* as an aesthetic event, not an inner state

Bharata Muni's *Nāṭyaśāstra* (composed sometime between roughly 200 BCE and 200 CE) sets
out eight *rasas* — the erotic (*śṛṅgāra*), comic (*hāsya*), pathetic/compassionate
(*karuṇā*), furious (*raudra*), heroic (*vīra*), fearful (*bhayānaka*), odious
(*bībhatsa*), and marvelous (*adbhuta*) — each produced in an audience by the
corresponding stable emotion (*bhāva*) of the character portrayed: delight, laughter,
sorrow, anger, heroism, fear, disgust, wonder
([overview of the *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s rasa theory](https://caleidoscope.in/featured/rasas-in-bharata-munis-natya-shastra);
[Lucknow University course notes on Bharata's rasa theory](https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202004120632194631nishi_Rasa_Theory.pdf)).
**This is a different kind of category than anything else on this timeline**: a *rasa* is
not, in the first instance, a state inside a person's body. It is what a well-made
performance produces in a spectator — an aesthetic "flavor" (the literal sense of
*rasa*) distilled and generalized out of a character's felt emotion. The unit of analysis
is the relationship between performer, text, and audience, not an individual's
physiology.

Roughly a millennium later, the Kashmiri philosopher Abhinavagupta, commenting on the
*Nāṭyaśāstra* in his *Abhinavabhāratī*, argued that Bharata's eight were incomplete and
added a ninth, *śānta* (peace, tranquility) — and, characteristically for a thinker
working in a nondual Śaiva framework, argued that *śānta* is not merely one *rasa* among
nine but the ground the other eight relish against, likened to the string that gives a
jeweled necklace its form even though no one admires the string itself
([summary of Abhinavagupta's addition of śānta-rasa](https://ijcrt.org/papers/IJCRT2010483.pdf)).
**Live scholarly point, not settled by this document:** whether *śānta* is best read as a
ninth peer category or as something closer to a substrate underneath the other eight is
itself contested within the tradition — a structural echo, worth naming without
overstating the parallel, of this library's own claim that constructed emotion is a
frame rather than a peer school.

### Classical China: *qing* and the seven emotions of the viscera

Chinese medical and philosophical texts group feeling under *qing* (情), a term broader
than the English "emotion" — it also carries senses closer to "circumstance,"
"disposition," or "the facts of a matter" in classical usage, which means the container
word itself does not partition cleanly along the same lines "emotion" does in English.
**This point about *qing*'s broader semantic range is drawn from general sinological
background rather than a single source checked in this drafting session; it should be
verified against a specialist source (e.g., A. C. Graham's or Michael Puett's work on
classical Chinese moral psychology) before being treated as settled.**

What is well documented is the clinical taxonomy: the *Huangdi Neijing* (Yellow
Emperor's Inner Classic, compiled roughly during the Han dynasty, 206 BCE–220 CE) and
later texts organize feeling into the "seven emotions" (*qīqíng*, 七情) — joy, anger,
worry, pensiveness/contemplation, sadness/grief, fear, and fright/shock — each assigned a
correspondence with one of the five viscera (*zang*): the heart governs joy, the liver
governs anger, the spleen governs contemplation, the lung governs grief, the kidney
governs fear ([TCM Wiki overview of the seven
emotions](https://tcmwiki.com/wiki/seven-emotions); [Asante Academy encyclopedia entry on
the seven emotions](https://asante-academy.com/encyclopedia/seven-emotions/)). The
organizing axis is neither valence nor Aristotle's judgment-alteration nor Bharata's
performer–audience relation: it is a physiological correspondence system, where an
emotion in excess is a *pathogenic factor* — too much anger injures the liver, too much
grief injures the lung — folding feeling directly into a theory of disease rather than a
theory of mind.

### Medieval Christendom: passions ordered by *arduousness*, not pleasantness

Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* (Prima Secundae, Questions 22–48), inherits
Aristotle's *pathē* by way of the Latin *passiones* and reorganizes them around the
soul's two sensitive appetites. The concupiscible appetite responds to sensible good or
evil taken simply, and yields six passions in three pairs: love/hatred, desire/aversion,
joy/sorrow. The irascible appetite responds to good or evil apprehended as *arduous* —
difficult to attain or avoid — and yields hope/despair and fear/daring, plus anger, which
Aquinas holds has no contrary passion because it responds only to an evil already present
and resisted ([*Summa Theologiae* I-II, Q.23, "How the passions differ from one
another"](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2023.htm)). Eleven passions in total. **Worth
stating plainly, since it is easy to read past:** the organizing axis is *not* valence.
Hope and fear sit in the same (irascible) family as daring and despair not because they
feel similarly pleasant or unpleasant but because they all concern a good or evil that is
hard to reach — the classification is about the *structure of the appetite's relation to
its object*, not about how the feeling registers subjectively. A taxonomy this different
from a valence axis existing at the intellectual center of medieval Christian moral
psychology for several centuries is itself evidence against the idea that "pleasant vs.
unpleasant" is a natural, self-evident organizing principle for feeling.

### Early modern Europe: three incompatible "basic" lists in one century

**Descartes**, in *Les passions de l'âme* (1649) — his last published work, written
directly in French rather than Latin — devotes the treatise's central section (roughly
Articles 51–148) to "the number and order of the passions" and arrives at six he calls
*primitive*: wonder (*admiration*), love, hatred, desire, joy, and sadness, from which
every other passion is compounded
([overview of the *Passions de l'âme*'s structure](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Passions_de_l%27%C3%A2me)).
**This is a claim worth making explicit and precise, because it is easy for readers
coming from contemporary psychology to conflate it with Ekman's list three centuries
later: Descartes' six are not Ekman's six.** Descartes has wonder and desire; Ekman has
surprise, disgust, and fear. Only love/joy and hatred/sadness are even loosely analogous,
and Descartes is explicit that his six are *causal primitives* — building blocks combined
by the mechanics of the pineal gland and the animal spirits — not a claim about universal
cross-cultural recognition. The point for this genealogy is narrower and firmer: **the
idea of "six basic passions/emotions" has a European philosophical pedigree that predates
Ekman by roughly three hundred years, and it is not the same six.** Anyone invoking "six
basics" as though the number itself carries evidential weight should notice that the
number has been six before, for entirely different reasons, with an entirely different
membership.

**Spinoza**, writing his *Ethics* (published posthumously 1677) in explicit, often
critical dialogue with Descartes, reduces the primitives further, to three: desire
(*cupiditas*), joy (*laetitia*), and sadness (*tristitia*) — defined not as felt qualities
but as transitions in the body's power of acting, joy being the passage to greater
perfection (greater power of acting) and sadness the passage to lesser
([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on Spinoza's theory of the
emotions](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD5Spinoza.html)). Every
other affect — love, hate, hope, fear, pride — is one of these three affects entangled
with the idea of an external cause. Three primitives, not six, and defined
metaphysically rather than phenomenologically.

**Hume**, in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), splits the passions along a different
seam entirely: *direct* passions (desire, aversion, grief, joy, hope, fear) arise
immediately from pleasure or pain, while *indirect* passions (pride, humility, love,
hatred) require, in addition, an idea of self or of another person as the passion's
object ([SEP on Hume's theory of the passions](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD8Hume.html)).
Pride and humility take the self as object; love and hatred take another person. Neither
Descartes' causal-primitive axis nor Spinoza's perfection-axis nor Aquinas's
arduousness-axis appears here — Hume's cut is about *what the passion is about*.

Three major European thinkers, working within roughly ninety years of each other, in the
same broad philosophical conversation, produced three structurally incompatible
taxonomies with three different primitive counts (six, three, and an unbounded set
divided by object-relation). **This is the clearest evidence in the pre-modern record
that "the basic emotions" is not a discovery converged upon from different angles — it is
a design choice, remade by each thinker to fit the argument they were building.**

### The nineteenth-century rupture: how "emotion" itself was made

This is the load-bearing claim of the whole genealogy, and it deserves to be stated
carefully rather than summarized into a slogan. Thomas Dixon's *From Passions to
Emotions: The Creation of a Secular Psychological Category* (Cambridge University Press,
2003) argues that "the emotions," as a single unifying English category covering
everything from a startle reflex to grief to religious rapture, is a nineteenth-century
invention — not a discovery of something that had always been there under older names.
Before this period, English psychological and theological vocabulary distinguished
**passions** (strong, often morally dangerous movements of the sensitive appetite, in the
Aristotelian-Thomist lineage), **affections** (steadier, often morally approved
inclinations — love of God, natural affection for kin), and **sentiments** (Scottish
Enlightenment vocabulary for refined, socially calibrated feelings, as in Adam Smith and
Hume). Dixon traces how Scottish thinkers — Hume himself, but especially Thomas Brown and
Thomas Chalmers — began collapsing these into a single genus, and how physicalist,
increasingly secular writers after them (Alexander Bain, Herbert Spencer, and eventually
Darwin) completed the consolidation, folding what had been morally and theologically
differentiated categories into one supposedly neutral, scientifically tractable class:
"the emotions" ([Cambridge University Press book
page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/from-passions-to-emotions/9E152739407029F882D0384D0670E49F);
[Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences
review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jhbs.20028)).

Dixon's own assessment of the cost, per the publisher's synopsis and reviews consulted
for this document, is that the older vocabulary made distinctions that "the emotions"
now flattens — a **passion** (something one *suffers*, is *passive* under) is not the
same kind of thing as an **affection** (something closer to a settled disposition of
love) or a **sentiment** (a feeling calibrated by moral judgment and social context), and
treating them as instances of one genus "hampers attempts to argue with any subtlety
about the enormous range of mental states and stances of which humans are capable."
**This document's synthesis, stated as such:** if Dixon is right, then the very container
that twentieth-century psychology set out to find universal biological markers *for* is
itself a relatively recent, English-language, secularizing artifact — which means
universality claims about the contents of that container are being asked to do work
before the container's own boundaries have been justified. This is not a claim that
"emotion" is meaningless or that other languages/eras had nothing comparable; it is a
claim that the specific *carving* — one undifferentiated genus rather than three or four
differentiated ones — needs its own defense, and rarely gets one.

### Darwin and James: two rival late-nineteenth-century foundations

Charles Darwin's *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) — originally
planned as a chapter of *The Descent of Man* before outgrowing it — argues that facial
and bodily expressions are largely innate and adaptive, evidenced by their appearance in
infants, in people born blind, and in nonhuman animals, none of whom could have learned
the expressions by cultural imitation
([Wikipedia summary of the book's argument and method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Expression_of_the_Emotions_in_Man_and_Animals)).
Darwin supplemented direct observation and Duchenne de Boulogne's photographic studies of
facial musculature with a network of questionnaires sent to missionaries and colonial
travelers, asking whether specific expressions appeared among the populations they
worked with — an early, methodologically loose ancestor of the cross-cultural
"universality" study design that Ekman would formalize a century later.

William James, in the 1884 essay "What Is an Emotion?" and later in *The Principles of
Psychology* (1890), proposed the opposite causal order from ordinary intuition: we do not
tremble because we are afraid; we are afraid because we tremble. Bodily changes — visceral,
postural, facial — come first, and the *feeling* of those bodily changes, James argued, is
the emotion; strip away the bodily feedback and "we find we have nothing left behind, no
'mind-stuff' out of which the emotion can be constituted"
([full text of "What Is an Emotion?" at York University's Classics in the History of
Psychology archive](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/emotion.htm)). James's theory —
independently proposed by the Danish physiologist Carl Lange around the same time, hence
"James–Lange theory" — is a direct ancestor of the interoception-forward accounts (Barrett
among them) that reappear at the end of this timeline, though James located emotion in
*specific, differentiated* bodily patterns per emotion, where the later constructionist
account locates it in a much more general, undifferentiated core-affect signal that gets
categorized after the fact.

### Twentieth century: Tomkins, Ekman, Plutchik, and the affect-programme model

Silvan Tomkins's *Affect Imagery Consciousness* (1962–63) proposed nine innate affects —
interest–excitement, enjoyment–joy, surprise–startle, fear–terror, distress–anguish,
anger–rage, shame, disgust, and "dissmell" — as hard-wired subcortical programs, largely
independent of the drives (hunger, sex) they had traditionally been subordinated to, and
argued that affect, not drive, is the primary motivator of behavior
([overview of Tomkins's affect theory and the nine affects](https://neuroanthropology.net/2008/06/23/affect-at-the-interface-silvan-tomkins/)).
Tomkins mentored a generation of researchers — Paul Ekman, Carroll Izard, Donald Nathanson
among them — who took the "hard-wired, universal, discrete affect program" idea forward
into cross-cultural facial-expression research.

Ekman narrowed Tomkins's nine down (in most published statements) to six — anger,
disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise — argued to be universal across cultures,
and, with Wallace Friesen, built the **Facial Action Coding System (FACS)**, published in
1978, an anatomically grounded notation for every visible facial-muscle movement,
building on and superseding a 1970 system by Carl-Herman Hjortsjö
([Paul Ekman's own account of FACS's history](https://paulekman.medium.com/the-history-of-the-facial-action-coding-system-facs-8787a86ed81f)).
Philosopher Paul Griffiths, synthesizing Ekman's account, named the resulting theoretical
package the **affect-programme model**: an automatic appraisal mechanism triggers one of a
small set of discrete, coordinated affect programs, each producing a characteristic
facial, vocal, and autonomic signature, subject to learned cultural "display rules" that
can mask but not eliminate the underlying program
([overview of Ekman's neurocultural theory and Griffiths's affect-programme
synthesis](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.7208/9780226488738-003/html)).

Robert Plutchik, working independently within a psychoevolutionary framework, proposed a
different set of eight — joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation
— arranged in 1980 as a color wheel of four opposite pairs (joy/sadness, trust/disgust,
fear/anger, anticipation/surprise), with intensity increasing toward the center and
combinations ("dyads") at the wheel's outer edges
([overview of Plutchik's wheel and psychoevolutionary theory](https://www.6seconds.org/2025/02/06/plutchik-wheel-emotions/)).
**Worth flagging plainly:** Plutchik's eight and Ekman's six overlap on five terms (joy,
fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger — six, if trust and anticipation are set aside)
but are not the same list, built from different theoretical starting points (evolutionary
adaptive function for Plutchik, facial universality data for Ekman), published within a
few years of each other. Even inside the mid-twentieth-century "basic emotions"
consensus, there was no actual consensus on the count or the membership — only on the
architecture (discrete, universal, biologically basic categories).

### Late twentieth and twenty-first century: the dimensional and constructionist turn

James Russell's 1980 circumplex model discarded discrete categories altogether in favor
of two continuous, bipolar dimensions — pleasure/displeasure and arousal/sleepiness —
onto which any feeling word can be plotted as a point rather than sorted into a bin
([overview of the circumplex model](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Motivation_and_emotion/Book/2019/Circumplex_model_of_affect)).
In 1994, Russell published a review directly challenging the evidentiary basis of the
"universal recognition" literature — "Is There Universal Recognition of Emotion from
Facial Expression? A Review of the Cross-Cultural Studies," *Psychological Bulletin* —
arguing that the apparent universality was substantially a product of study design,
a point taken up and extended by Barrett's group two decades later (see Part B).

Lisa Feldman Barrett's **theory of constructed emotion** (developed from the earlier
"conceptual act theory," synthesized in *How Emotions Are Made*, 2017) proposes that
emotion is not a triggered program but a real-time construction: the brain continuously
generates a general, undifferentiated **core affect** signal (a point in
valence–arousal space, descended from Russell's circumplex) from interoceptive and
exteroceptive input, and *categorizes* that signal — in the moment, using concepts
learned from language and culture — into an instance of "fear" or "shame" or whatever
concept the situation and the person's conceptual repertoire make available
([Wikipedia overview of the theory of constructed
emotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_constructed_emotion); [Barrett's own
description of *How Emotions Are Made*](https://lisafeldmanbarrett.com/books/how-emotions-are-made/)).
On this account, a person with a finer-grained emotional vocabulary can construct (and
therefore later regulate) finer-grained emotional instances — the "emotional granularity"
research the library's own README cites as evidence the constructed account is not merely
deflationary.

Ruth Leys's *The Ascent of Affect: Genealogy and Critique* (University of Chicago Press,
2017) is not itself a theory of emotion but a critical history of the field since roughly
the 1960s, tracing a recurring, unresolved split between camps that emphasize the
*intentionality* or meaning of emotions (but then struggle to explain emotion in
non-human animals or infants, who plausibly lack rich intentional content) and camps that
emphasize *universality and automaticity* (but then struggle to explain how emotions
carry meaning at all)
([American Historical Review review of Leys](https://academic.oup.com/ahr/article/125/1/197/5721709);
[publisher's description](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/A/bo26773949.html)).
Leys is a useful check on any tidy origin story — including this one — because her
argument is that the field has oscillated between these poles for sixty years without
resolving the tension, which means the constructionist account should be read as the
current strongest position in an ongoing argument, not as the argument's last word.

## Part B — Why did "six universal emotions" become consensus?

### The forced-choice paradigm: a design that cannot fail to find agreement

Ekman and Friesen's landmark studies with the Fore of Papua New Guinea (late 1960s–1971,
published as "Constants Across Cultures in the Face and Emotion") are usually summarized
as showing that an isolated, pre-literate culture with no exposure to Western media could
correctly match facial photographs to emotions at well-above-chance rates. **Established,
and worth stating precisely, because the precision is exactly what the method obscures:**
the task was not "look at this face and tell us what it shows." It was: for each of 30
photographs, choose the one word, from a short supplied list (typically the six target
emotion words, sometimes embedded in a short story), that best fits
([overview of the forced-choice design and its critiques](https://thinkingfeelingbeing.com/2025/08/24/the-science-of-facial-expressions-paul-ekman-universality-and-the-debate-over-emotion/)).
A forced-choice design of this kind cannot, by construction, tell you whether a culture
independently carves feeling into those six categories — it can only tell you whether,
*given* those six categories as options, people can match faces to them at rates better
than random guessing among six alternatives. Even a fairly weak, partial correspondence
between a culture's own concepts and the supplied English-derived categories will clear
that bar. The Fore data itself already contained a tell: participants reliably confused
fear and surprise with each other — a confusion that a truly discrete, universal six-way
system should not produce, and one that would resurface as a live issue decades later.

By contrast, **free-labelling** designs (show a face, ask the participant to say — in
their own words, with no list — what it shows) and **free-sorting** designs (show a set
of faces or sounds, ask the participant to group them however seems natural, with no
predetermined number of groups) do not furnish the categories in advance. Agreement rates
on free-labelling tasks, even within single Western populations, are substantially lower
than forced-choice agreement rates — a gap the forced-choice literature had already
noted internally well before Barrett's group made it central to their critique
(see the PubMed listing for ["The forced-choice paradigm and the perception of facial
expressions of emotion"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11195893/), an early paper
specifically interrogating this gap).

### Gendron and Barrett's Himba studies

Maria Gendron, working in Lisa Feldman Barrett's lab, spent eighteen days with the Himba
of Namibia — a population with minimal exposure to Western media — and ran a free-sorting
task: participants were shown photographs of posed facial expressions (the same six
Ekman had used) and asked to sort them into piles, with no constraint on how many piles or
what basis to sort by. Rather than six neat emotion-based piles, Himba participants
produced varying numbers of groups, with the same image sometimes appearing in more than
one pile across participants, and — critically — subsequent analysis (multidimensional
scaling) found that Himba sorting was organized substantially around *behavior and
situation* rather than around the six posited emotion categories; a parallel free-sort
run with Boston participants produced much closer-to-consensus, category-like groupings
([Gendron, Roberson, van der Vyver & Barrett, "Perceptions of Emotion From Facial
Expressions Are Not Culturally Universal: Evidence From a Remote Culture," *Emotion*,
2014, full text](https://affective-science.org/pubs/2014/gendron-himba-emotion-2014.pdf);
[TIME magazine's summary of the fieldwork](https://time.com/14478/emotions-may-not-be-so-universal-after-all/)).
A companion free-sort with vocal emotional sounds found the same pattern — the identical
sound was heard as joyful by some Himba listeners and as more negative by others, with far
less consensus than the Boston sample produced. Gendron and Barrett's own reading, laid
out more fully in a follow-up review ("Universality Reconsidered: Diversity in Making
Meaning of Facial Expressions," *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 2018,
[full text](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0963721417746794)), is that the
apparent universal consensus the forced-choice literature had reported since the 1970s
was substantially an artifact of a study design that hands participants the categories
before asking whether they use them.

### The stronger claim, and where it becomes a live dispute rather than a settled finding

Barrett's fuller position — most compactly stated in Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella,
Martinez & Pollak, "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered: Challenges to Inferring Emotion
From Human Facial Movements," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2019 (the
paper the README already cites) — goes beyond "the forced-choice method inflates
agreement" to a broader claim: across the twentieth century, a large fraction of the
cross-cultural "universal recognition" literature effectively **supplied research
participants with the emotion categories it later reported having discovered in them**,
because posed photographs, a short label list, and a forced choice among those labels
were the standard instrument, repeated across hundreds of studies, with methodological
variants (free labelling, free sorting, spontaneous rather than posed expressions)
appearing far less often and, when they did appear, generally showing weaker or more
context-dependent agreement
([the PSPI paper's abstract and scope, via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856/)).

**This is presented here as Barrett's position, not as this document's settled verdict,
because it is a live scientific dispute and Ekman and his collaborators have responded
substantively rather than conceding.** Ekman replied as early as 1994 to Russell's initial
methodological critique with new data he argued answered it, and the exchange has
continued since; contemporary defenders of at least a qualified universality thesis point
to converging evidence that does not depend on the forced-choice design specifically:
Elfenbein and Ambady's 2002 meta-analysis found that, across dozens of cross-cultural
studies using varied methods, emotion recognition was reliably better than chance across
cultural boundaries, even as it also documented a robust "in-group advantage" (recognition
is more accurate within one's own cultural group than across groups) — read by Barrett's
side as evidence *against* strict universality and by Ekman's side as evidence *for* a
universal core modulated by cultural "dialects"
([Elfenbein & Ambady, "On the Universality and Cultural Specificity of Emotion
Recognition: A Meta-Analysis," *Psychological Bulletin*, 2002](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/on-the-universality-and-cultural-specificity-of-emotion-recogniti/)).
Matsumoto and Willingham's 2009 study of congenitally blind judo athletes at the 2004
Olympic and Paralympic Games found no measurable difference between blind and sighted
athletes' spontaneous facial expressions of victory and defeat, which is hard to explain
if those specific expressions were purely the product of visual observational learning —
though it also documented a learned component in how athletes subsequently *managed* or
masked those expressions socially, which is itself consistent with the "display rules"
half of Ekman's own neurocultural theory
([summary of the Matsumoto & Willingham findings](https://blogs.scientificamerican.com/psysociety/blind-athletes-provide-clues-about-the-nature-of-our-emotions/)).
Cordaro, Keltner, and colleagues have since published cross-cultural work extending
beyond the face to vocal bursts and body posture across a wider set of cultures,
including remote populations, reporting recognition patterns for a larger set of
expressions than Ekman's original six — work this document was not able to verify in
full citation detail during drafting and flags accordingly below.

The honest state of the dispute, as best this document can represent it: there is broad
agreement that *some* facial configurations are recognized above chance across most
tested cultures under most tested conditions, and broad agreement that agreement rates
are highly sensitive to method (posed vs. spontaneous stimuli, forced-choice vs. free
response, in-group vs. out-group judges). What remains genuinely contested is whether
that residual, method-sensitive, partial cross-cultural agreement is best explained by
Ekman's discrete, universal affect programs modulated by cultural display rules, or by
Barrett's constructed-emotion account, in which a smaller set of genuinely universal
ingredients (core affect, and perhaps a handful of very coarse valence/arousal-linked
facial or vocal signals) get built into culturally specific categories that happen to
resemble each other more than chance would predict, without any of them being
"basic" in Ekman's sense.

### Why it spread anyway: infrastructure outruns hypothesis

**This document's synthesis:** a scientific claim that becomes infrastructure is
harder to dislodge than one that stays a journal-article hypothesis, because dislodging
it now costs something beyond intellectual consistency. Basic-emotion theory became
infrastructure on at least four fronts:

- **FACS itself.** Published in 1978 and still the standard tool for coding facial
  movement in emotion research, FACS is method-neutral at the level of muscle
  measurement — it just names which action units moved — but it was built, and is still
  overwhelmingly used, inside a research program that assumes a small set of discrete
  target emotions to code *for*
  ([Ekman's own history of FACS](https://paulekman.medium.com/the-history-of-the-facial-action-coding-system-facs-8787a86ed81f)).
- **Security and deception-detection programs.** The U.S. Transportation Security
  Administration's SPOT program (Screening of Passengers by Observation Techniques),
  deployed from 2007, trained roughly 3,000 Behavior Detection Officers on
  microexpression-based indicators drawing on Ekman's work, and spent an estimated $900
  million in its first six years. A 2013 U.S. Government Accountability Office report
  (GAO-14-159) found no scientifically validated evidence that the behavioral indicators
  used could reliably identify security threats, and recommended Congress weigh that
  against continued funding
  ([Wikipedia, "BDA (TSA program)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BDA_(TSA_program));
  [Paul Ekman Group's own response to the GAO
  finding](https://www.paulekman.com/blog/ineffectively-testing-the-effectiveness-of-tsa/)).
  A theory with a $900-million-and-counting government program built on it has powerful
  institutional reasons not to be quietly revised.
- **Affective computing and automated "emotion recognition."** A large commercial
  industry — marketing analytics, hiring-screen tools, driver-monitoring systems,
  "aggression detectors" — infers emotional state from facial video, largely on the
  assumption that a small set of discrete, visually legible categories exist to be
  detected. The American Civil Liberties Union and multiple researchers have argued this
  industry runs ahead of the science it claims to rest on; the recruiting-technology firm
  HireVue announced it would stop using facial-visual analysis in its algorithms, and
  Microsoft's Face API documentation now explicitly states that facial expressions alone
  do not reliably represent internal emotional states
  ([ACLU, "Experts Say 'Emotion Recognition' Lacks Scientific
  Foundation"](https://www.aclu.org/news/privacy-technology/experts-say-emotion-recognition-lacks-scientific)).
  Products, once shipped, create their own momentum independent of the evidence for the
  theory they're built on.
- **Popular culture.** Paul Ekman consulted directly on Pixar's *Inside Out* (2015),
  whose five character-emotions (joy, sadness, anger, fear, disgust — a subset of his
  six, minus surprise) are explicitly drawn from his research program and have since
  become, for an entire generation of children and the parents who watched the film with
  them, the default folk taxonomy of feeling
  ([background on Ekman's consulting role](https://www.paulekman.com/projects/inside-out/)).
  Ekman also served as scientific consultant to the Fox drama *Lie to Me* (2009–2011),
  whose premise — a protagonist who reads concealed emotion and deception from
  microexpressions — dramatizes the affect-programme model as reliable, near-real-time
  lie detection; Ekman himself has stated the show is "about 85 percent accurate" to the
  underlying science while cautioning that it compresses timelines and overstates
  certainty for dramatic effect
  ([Paul Ekman Group's own account of the show's basis and its
  liberties](https://www.paulekman.com/projects/lie-to-me/)).

None of these four is itself evidence for or against the theory. That is precisely the
point: infrastructure and popularity are orthogonal to truth, but they are not orthogonal
to how hard a theory is to revise once it is load-bearing for a $900-million security
program, a hiring-screen industry, and a movie every literate parent in the anglophone
world has shown their child.

### What basic-emotion theory got right

A genuine reckoning, not a hit piece, has to say this plainly: basic-emotion theory
generated an enormous amount of real, useful, falsifiable science, and some of its
central claims have held up under methods its own critics designed. Darwin's and later
Ekman's prediction that *some* facial patterns would be recognized above chance across
unrelated cultures has survived Russell's and Barrett's critiques in weakened but real
form — Elfenbein and Ambady's meta-analysis found above-chance cross-cultural recognition
even while documenting the in-group advantage that complicates strict universality.
Matsumoto and Willingham's blind-athlete data is difficult to explain without *some*
innate, non-visually-learned component to at least a subset of spontaneous facial
expression. FACS, whatever theory it was built to serve, is a genuinely rigorous,
theory-agnostic measurement tool that constructionist researchers (Barrett's own group
included) continue to use, because a precise vocabulary for facial movement is valuable
independent of which theory of emotion category formation turns out to be right. And the
affect-programme model's core intuition — that some emotional responses are fast,
involuntary, and only partially under cultural or deliberate control — is not something
the constructed-emotion account actually denies; Barrett's theory relocates *where* the
construction happens (in real time, from core affect plus concept) without claiming that
emotional response is infinitely plastic or entirely under conscious control moment to
moment.

### This document's best answer to "why did six become consensus"

**Stated plainly, as this document's own synthesis, not a quotation from any single
source:** "six universal emotions" became consensus because a forced-choice methodology
that could not help but manufacture the appearance of cross-cultural agreement was
applied, with genuine rigor and good faith, across hundreds of studies before its
weaker alternatives (free labelling, free sorting, spontaneous rather than posed
stimuli) were tried seriously at scale — and because, once the theory had a precise
measurement tool (FACS), a security-policy application, a criminal-justice and
deception-detection application, a booming commercial-AI application, and a beloved
animated film, the cost of revising it stopped being purely intellectual. None of that
makes the theory false. It makes the theory's *popularity* a poor proxy for its truth,
and it means the burden of proof for "these six, and only these six, are what feeling
is" was met by momentum more than by the free-response and free-sorting evidence that
would actually settle the question — evidence which, where it has been gathered, points
toward something closer to Barrett's constructed account: real, partial, method-sensitive
cross-cultural agreement on a small set of signals, built into culturally variable
categories, rather than six universal joints at which feeling was always going to be
carved.

## Part C — Translation standards: how to avoid the trap this library is built to avoid

Everything in Part B is, at bottom, a translation problem: a researcher walks into a
culture with a set of English words already in hand (fear, anger, disgust...) and asks
whether that culture's feelings match. The danger is not really about facial expressions
specifically — it is about what happens whenever anyone tries to move a concept across a
linguistic or cultural boundary and assumes, rather than checks, that the target has an
equivalent waiting for it.

### Controlled equivocation: the danger is not missing the match, it's assuming you found one

The Brazilian anthropologist Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, in "Perspectival Anthropology
and the Method of Controlled Equivocation" (a 2004 keynote later published in *Tipití:
Journal of the Society for the Anthropology of Lowland South America*), argues that doing
anthropology *is* comparing anthropologies — comparing, that is, the ontological
commitments embedded in one's own analytic vocabulary against those embedded in the
vocabulary of the people being studied, rather than assuming a shared world that the two
vocabularies merely describe differently
([full text via Trinity University's digital
commons](https://digitalcommons.trinity.edu/tipiti/vol2/iss1/1/)). His central claim: an
*equivocation* — using the same word for what are, on inspection, two different concepts
rooted in two different worlds — is not a translation error to be eliminated. It is,
Viveiros de Castro argues, structurally unavoidable whenever translation across
sufficiently different conceptual schemes happens at all; the discipline's job is not to
pretend equivocation away but to **locate and control it** — to make visible, at each
point of translation, exactly where the two vocabularies diverge, rather than silently
picking one side's meaning and calling it the shared, neutral one.

This connects directly to Viveiros de Castro's better-known concept, Amerindian
**perspectivism**: the claim, drawn from ethnographic work across Amazonian cosmologies,
that different kinds of beings (humans, jaguars, spirits) are not understood, within these
cosmologies, as holding different *views* of one shared world — they are understood as
inhabiting genuinely different worlds, produced by their different bodies, while sharing
a single generic "culture" or subjective viewpoint (what a jaguar sees as manioc beer, a
human sees as blood; both are, from each perspective, the correct and only drink). This is
"multinaturalism" rather than multiculturalism: one culture, many natures, rather than
one nature described by many cultures
([overview of Amerindian perspectivism and multinaturalism](https://ea.fflch.usp.br/en/concept/amerindian-perspectivism)).

**This document's own synthesis, explicitly flagged as such and not a claim advanced by
Viveiros de Castro, Barrett, or Hoffman about each other's work:** perspectivism, Barrett's
constructionism, and the cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman's *interface theory of
perception* are making a structurally similar move from three entirely different
disciplinary starting points. Hoffman's interface theory — built on the mathematically
formalized "Fitness-Beats-Truth" theorem — argues that natural selection favors perceptual
systems tuned to fitness (whatever helps an organism act effectively) over perceptual
systems tuned to truth (whatever accurately represents mind-independent reality), and that
these two are not the same target except by coincidence; what we perceive is closer to a
species-relative desktop interface than a transparent window onto the world as it actually
is ([overview of the Interface Theory of Perception and the Fitness-Beats-Truth
theorem](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-015-0890-8)). Barrett's
constructed-emotion account makes the same shape of move one level in: what a person
*feels* is not a transparent readout of a pre-existing biological category but a
real-time construction, built from more general raw material (core affect) using
concepts available to that specific person in that specific culture and moment.
Viveiros de Castro's perspectivism makes the move at the level of ontology itself: what a
being *perceives as the world* is relative to the kind of body it has, not a shared
neutral backdrop everyone is merely describing differently. All three are versions of the
claim that what appears — a face read as "angry," a world seen as blood-red or beer-gold,
a scene perceived as full of solid objects in Euclidean space — is an interface relative
to the perceiver's kind, not a transparent window onto a perceiver-independent given. This
parallel is offered here as this library's own interpretive synthesis across three
literatures that do not cite each other and were not built to agree; it should not be
read as a claim that Viveiros de Castro, Barrett, or Hoffman would endorse the comparison,
and a reader who wants to use the parallel for anything beyond orientation should go back
to each source and check where the analogy breaks down (Hoffman's claim is about
perception of a physical environment shaped by natural selection; Barrett's is about
categorization of internal affective states shaped by cultural learning; Viveiros de
Castro's is an ethnographic-ontological claim about how specific Amazonian cosmologies
organize being, not a general cognitive-science thesis). The family resemblance is real
and worth naming. It is not an identity.

### Wierzbicka's Natural Semantic Metalanguage: the rigorous answer to the maintainer's worry

If the danger in cross-cultural emotion research is smuggling in English folk categories
and calling them neutral scientific instruments, the linguist Anna Wierzbicka's decades-long
project offers what may be the most methodologically disciplined available response.
Wierzbicka's core claim is blunt: "anger," "fear," "disgust," "sadness," and "happiness"
are **English words**, carrying the specific conceptual commitments of English folk
psychology, and using them as the analytic categories for a cross-linguistic or
cross-cultural study of emotion — rather than as one language's data point among many —
quietly imports those commitments as if they were culture-neutral science
([overview of Wierzbicka's argument against emotion-word universals](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315013205_Emotions_across_Linguistic_Theories_A_Wierzbicka_Emotions_across_Languages_and_Cultures_Diversity_and_Universals)).
Her proposed solution, developed with Cliff Goddard since the mid-1980s as the **Natural
Semantic Metalanguage (NSM)**, is to define emotion concepts — in any language, including
English — using only a small, empirically tested set of **semantic primes**: simple
concepts (currently numbering around 65 — "good," "bad," "do," "happen," "want," "feel,"
"think," "know," among others) argued, on the basis of translatability testing across more
than thirty typologically diverse languages, to have direct lexical or grammatical
exponents in every language tested
([overview of NSM's semantic primes and their cross-linguistic
testing](https://caseykeith.me/semantic-primes/); [Wierzbicka's own retrospective, "'Semantic
Primitives,' Fifty Years Later"](https://journals.rudn.ru/linguistics/article/view/26795)).
On this method, "anger" is not treated as a universal box to be filled or emptied by
different cultures; instead, the *English* concept "anger" gets its own explicit definition
built from primes (something like: "someone thinks something bad happened, this person
did something bad, I don't want this, I feel something bad because of this"), and a
comparable Ifaluk or Ilongot or Japanese concept gets its *own* prime-built definition,
checked against native-speaker judgment, allowing the two definitions to be compared
explicitly for where they converge and diverge — rather than assuming convergence by
translating both words as "anger" and moving on.

**This document's assessment, offered as an argument rather than a settled fact:** NSM is
arguably *more* methodologically disciplined than much of the forced-choice quantitative
literature it implicitly critiques, precisely because it refuses to let a folk category do
double duty as both the object of study and the neutral measuring instrument. A
forced-choice study that hands a Himba participant the English-derived category "anger"
(translated, inevitably, through some Otjihimba word chosen by the researcher or a local
translator) and then reports "recognition" of anger has already made the equivocation
Viveiros de Castro warns about, and has not controlled for it. NSM's discipline of
building every emotion concept — English ones included — from the same small, tested
prime vocabulary is a structural safeguard against exactly that move, even though it is
slower, more labor-intensive, and produces results less easily converted into a six-way
forced-choice accuracy percentage.

### Lutz's Ifaluk: *fago* and *song* as worked examples

Catherine Lutz's *Unnatural Emotions: Everyday Sentiments on a Micronesian Atoll and
Their Challenge to Western Theory* (University of Chicago Press, 1988), based on
fieldwork on Ifaluk atoll in 1977–78, works through this problem concretely rather than
programmatically. Lutz analyzes several Ifaluk emotion concepts that resist clean mapping
onto English terms, most extensively ***fago*** and ***song***. *Fago* is often glossed
as "compassion," "love," or "sadness" depending on context, but the gloss undersells it:
*fago* is felt toward someone who is suffering, in need, or leaving — it binds compassion,
love, and a kind of grief together as a single concept in a way English simply does not,
because English's category boundaries were drawn along different lines. ***Song*** is
often glossed "anger," but Ifaluk *song* is specifically *justifiable* anger — anger felt
in response to another's misconduct, sanctioned and legible within Ifaluk moral order in
a way that undifferentiated English "anger" is not
([overview of Lutz's Ifaluk fieldwork and central
concepts](https://www.academia.edu/54274341/Unnatural_Emotions_Everyday_Sentiments_on_a_Micronesian_Atoll_and_Their_Challenge_to_Western_Theory_Catherine_A_Lutz); [book
review summarizing Lutz's ethnopsychological
framework](https://lir.byuh.edu/index.php/pacific/article/download/2748/2660)). The book's
title is deliberately ironic: Ifaluk emotions only look "unnatural" from a starting
assumption — Lutz's actual target — that English/Western emotion categories are the
natural, default baseline against which other cultures' categories are variations. Lutz's
broader argument is that Ifaluk emotion concepts are inseparable from Ifaluk theories of
self, relationship, and social obligation — *fago* and *song* are not private internal
states dressed in local vocabulary; they are *relational and moral* concepts that do not
survive being extracted from that context and relabeled with an English word chosen for
rough resemblance.

### A checklist of standards for this library

The practical upshot, for every school this library represents: before an item claims
that School X's term T means the same thing as this library's `emotion_key` for a given
feeling, or before content describes how "Culture Y classifies emotion Z," the following
should hold —

1. **Name the translation, don't erase it.** If a school's or culture's term is being
   mapped to a `metadata.emotion_key`, say explicitly that this is a mapping, not an
   identity — the way `metadata.emotion_key` is already documented in this repo's
   `CLAUDE.md` as "the cross-grammar matching key," not as a claim that the underlying
   feeling is the same thing across schools.
2. **Prefer the source tradition's own definition to a supplied English gloss.** Where
   primary or scholarly material gives a definition in the tradition's own terms (as
   Bharata defines *rasa* relationally, as Aquinas defines a passion by its faculty and
   object, as Lutz reports Ifaluk speakers glossing *fago*), present that definition
   before or instead of a one-word English equivalent.
3. **Flag false friends explicitly.** Where two traditions use superficially similar
   English labels for structurally different concepts — Sue Johnson's EFT vs. Leslie
   Greenberg's EFT is already a flagged case in this repo's `CLAUDE.md`; "six basic
   passions" (Descartes) vs. "six basic emotions" (Ekman) is another — say so in the
   content itself, not just in an internal note.
4. **Distinguish forced-choice-style evidence from free-response-style evidence.** When
   citing a study as evidence that a category is cross-culturally shared, note which kind
   of task produced the result. A forced-choice match-to-list result and a free-sort or
   free-label result are not interchangeable evidence, and presenting the former as if it
   were the latter is the exact error this document spends Part B unpacking.
5. **Hold evidence-tier honesty for cultural claims, not just clinical ones.** The
   `metadata.evidence_tier` convention this repo already applies to schools like EFT and
   Gottman's method (`independently-replicated` / `growing` / `observational-only` /
   `theoretical`) should be applied with the same rigor to claims about what a given
   culture's emotion vocabulary contains — a single ethnographer's fieldwork
   (`observational-only`, however rich) is a different evidence tier from a claim
   replicated across multiple independent fieldworkers or corroborated by NSM-style
   cross-linguistic testing.
6. **Keep the constructionist/perspectivist/interface parallel labeled as this library's
   synthesis.** The parallel drawn above between Barrett, Hoffman, and Viveiros de Castro
   is useful for orienting a reader inside this library's own framing. It should never be
   presented, in a school's own section, as something those three thinkers jointly hold —
   per this repo's `CLAUDE.md` rule that editorial reading belongs in a clearly marked
   `Constructionist lens` section, never inside a school's own voice.
7. **When in doubt, cite the fieldwork, not the summary of the fieldwork.** Multiple
   claims in this document (Gendron's exact sorting results on Himba; the precise
   current count of NSM primes; the full citation trail for Cordaro and Keltner's
   post-2016 cross-cultural expression work) were only available in this drafting
   session at one or two removes from the primary publication. Where that is true here,
   it is marked below. The same discipline should apply to every future grammar built
   from this research: mark the distance between a claim and its primary source, rather
   than letting a secondary summary quietly stand in for it.
